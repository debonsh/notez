Lesson · BT203 Unit 2 — Measurement & Production

Dynamometer — Types, Working Principle and Applications

A dynamometer measures force, torque or power, and RGPV asks for its types. That is the whole question — a definition, a classification into absorption and transmission types, and one example of each. It appeared in the December 2020 paper as Q2b and it is one of the cheapest 5 marks in the unit, so it is worth knowing cold rather than merely recognising.

The one-sentence statement

A dynamometer is an instrument for measuring force, torque or power. In its
force form it measures a load directly by the elastic extension of a spring
or by dead weights. In its power form it is classified into ABSORPTION
dynamometers, which absorb the power of the prime mover and dissipate it as
heat (Prony brake, rope brake, hydraulic, eddy-current), and TRANSMISSION
dynamometers, which measure the power being transmitted without absorbing it
(belt, torsion and epicyclic types).

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Ask whether the machine swallows the power or merely watches it go past — absorption or transmission — and the classification writes itself.”

The distinction that carries the marks

An absorption dynamometer must absorb all the power it measures, so that power is thrown away as heat — which is why these are laboratory instruments. A transmission dynamometer measures power in a drive that keeps running, so it can sit on a machine in service. If one sentence is written to show the classification is understood rather than memorised, that is the one.

Why a spring dynamometer must be calibrated. The spring's stiffness k changes with use and with temperature, so the scale cannot be trusted from first principles. The instrument is therefore calibrated against known dead weights, and the calibration is the only thing that makes the reading meaningful. A question that asks why a spring dynamometer needs calibration is asking for exactly that sentence.

Solved A — a spring dynamometer

A spring dynamometer has a spring of stiffness 500 N/m. Under load the spring extends by 120 mm. Find the force measured, and state why the instrument must be calibrated.

Given: k = 500 N/m, x = 120 mm. Find: F. Formula: F = kx. Solve:

x = 120 mm = 0.120 m        (convert to metres FIRST)

F = 500 x 0.120
  = 60.0 N

Answer: the force is 60.0 N.

Check: substituting back, 60.0/500 = 0.120 m, recovering the stated extension. Independent sense check: a stiffness of 500 N/m means 500 N would extend the spring a full metre, so a 120 mm extension giving 60 N is consistent with a spring of that softness. Had the extension been left in millimetres the answer would have come out 60000 N, which is five tonnes from a spring soft enough to stretch a metre — immediately absurd. Why calibration is needed: the stiffness k is not constant in service — it changes with age, with temperature and with overload, and it is not known to the accuracy the scale implies. The instrument is therefore calibrated against known dead weights, and without that calibration the graduation marks are only nominal.

Solved B — a Prony brake

A Prony brake carries a dead weight of 200 N at a lever arm of 0.6 m from the shaft axis. The shaft rotates at 400 rpm. Find the torque and the brake power.

Given: W = 200 N, L = 0.6 m, N = 400 rpm. Find: T and BP. Formula: T = WL; BP = 2πNT/60. Solve:

T = 200 x 0.6 = 120.0 N.m

BP = 2 x pi x 400 x 120 / 60
   = 301592.9 / 60
   = 5026.55 W
   = 5.03 kW

Answer: torque 120 N·m, brake power 5.03 kW.

Check: substituting back, 5026.55 × 60/(2π × 400) = 120.0 N·m, recovering the torque. Independent magnitude check: 5 kW is the output of a small single-cylinder engine, which is exactly the scale of machine a laboratory Prony brake is used on, and it also sits within the low-speed, low-power limit that the block friction imposes. Had the 60 been omitted, the power would have come out 60 times too large at about 302 kW, which is a large industrial engine and far beyond what a wooden-block brake can absorb.

Solved C — a rope brake, and the efficiency

A rope brake has a drum of 0.5 m diameter with a rope of 0.02 m diameter. The dead weight is 300 N and the spring balance reads 50 N. The shaft runs at 300 rpm. Find the torque and the brake power.

The net load is the difference between the two, because the spring balance is holding part of the weight up.

Given: W = 300 N, S = 50 N, D = 0.5 m, d = 0.02 m, N = 300 rpm. Find: T and BP. Formula: T = (W − S)(D + d)/2; BP = 2πNT/60. Solve:

net load = W - S = 300 - 50 = 250 N

effective radius = (D + d)/2 = (0.5 + 0.02)/2
                 = 0.26 m        <- the rope centre sits d/2 out
                                    from the drum surface

T = 250 x 0.26 = 65.0 N.m

BP = 2 x pi x 300 x 65 / 60
   = 122522.1 / 60
   = 2042.04 W
   = 2.04 kW

Answer: torque 65 N·m, brake power 2.04 kW.

Check: substituting back, 2042.04 × 60/(2π × 300) = 65.0 N·m, recovering the torque. Second check on the radius: the effective radius is (D + d)/2 = 0.26 m, not D/2 = 0.25 m, because the rope acts at its own centre line, half a rope diameter out from the drum surface. Using 0.25 m would give 62.5 N·m and 1.96 kW — a 4% error that is entirely avoidable and is the standard slip on this numeral. Third check on the net load: if the spring balance reads 50 N while the weight is 300 N, the brake is being loaded by the 250 N difference, and using the full 300 N would overstate the torque by 20%.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Convert to metres before substituting. A 120 mm extension must become 0.120 m, and a rope of 20 mm diameter must become 0.02 m.
  • The rope brake radius is (D + d)/2, not D/2. The rope acts at its own centre line, half a rope diameter outside the drum.
  • The rope brake net load is (W − S), not W. The spring balance supports part of the weight.
  • Remember the 2π/60 in BP = 2πNT/60. It converts rpm to radians per second; without it the power is wrong by a factor of about 9.55.
  • Torque in N·m gives power in watts. Divide by 1000 for kW, and state which you are reporting.
  • A Prony brake is limited to low speeds and small powers because the heat at the friction surface has nowhere to go. A calculated 300 kW from a Prony brake is a sign of an arithmetic slip, not a real machine.
  • The spring dynamometer's stiffness k changes in service, which is why the instrument needs calibration rather than trusting its scale.
  • Absorption dynamometers waste the power they measure; transmission types do not. Applying the wrong class to a machine in service is a conceptual error, not an arithmetic one.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “A dynamometer is an instrument used for measuring force, torque or power, and is classified according to whether it absorbs the power it measures or merely measures power being transmitted.”
  • Classification line — “Dynamometers are broadly of two kinds: absorption dynamometers, such as the Prony brake, rope brake, hydraulic and eddy-current types, which absorb the entire power and dissipate it as heat; and transmission dynamometers, such as the belt, torsion and epicyclic types, which measure the power transmitted without absorbing it.”
  • Force line — “In its simplest form the dynamometer measures force directly, either by the extension of a calibrated spring, F = kx, or by comparison with dead weights.”
  • Prony line — “In the Prony brake the torque is the dead weight multiplied by the lever arm, T = WL, and the brake power follows from BP = 2πNT/60.”
  • Limitation line — “The absorption types are limited by the heat generated at the friction or fluid surface, so they are laboratory instruments suited to low speeds and moderate powers.”
  • Closing line — “Hence the choice of dynamometer is decided by whether the power may be absorbed or must continue to the driven machine, which is why absorption types are used on test beds and transmission types on drives in service.”

Dynamometer — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

A dynamometer is an instrument for measuring force, torque or power.

In its FORCE form it measures a load directly by the elastic extension
of a calibrated spring (F = kx) or by comparison with dead weights.

In its POWER form it is classified as:

  ABSORPTION dynamometer - absorbs the entire power of the prime mover
    and dissipates it as heat. Limited by heat, hence laboratory use.
    Types: Prony brake, rope brake, hydraulic, eddy-current.

  TRANSMISSION dynamometer - measures power being transmitted WITHOUT
    absorbing it, so the drive keeps running.
    Types: belt, torsion, epicyclic.

  DRIVING dynamometer - a calibrated motor driving the machine under test.

THE TEST: does it take the power away, or only read it?

Derivation in four steps

1. Force     -> F = k x          [spring stiffness x extension]
2. Torque    -> T = W L                     (Prony brake)
                T = (W - S)(D + d)/2        (rope brake)
3. Power     -> BP = 2 pi N T / 60
                [2 pi / 60 converts rpm to rad/s]
4. Classify  -> absorption (takes the power) or transmission (reads it)

Definitions to reproduce

  • Dynamometer — an instrument for measuring force, torque or power; in the power form classified into absorption and transmission types.
  • Absorption dynamometer — one which absorbs the whole power of the prime mover and dissipates it as heat, so the power is lost to the machine.
  • Transmission dynamometer — one which measures the power transmitted without absorbing it, so the drive continues to the driven machine.
  • Prony brake — two friction blocks clamped round a brake drum, loaded through a lever arm and a dead weight; T = WL.
  • Rope brake — one or more ropes round a drum with a dead weight at one end and a spring balance at the other; T = (W − S)(D + d)/2.
  • Effective radius of a rope brake — (D + d)/2, since the rope acts along its own centre line, half a rope diameter outside the drum surface.
  • Brake power — the power measured at the output shaft, BP = 2πNT/60 with N in rpm and T in N·m.
  • Torsion dynamometer — a transmission dynamometer measuring the angle of twist of a running shaft, from which the torque is obtained.
  • Eddy-current dynamometer — an absorption dynamometer in which eddy currents induced in a rotating disc provide an electrically controllable load.

Numeral shortcut

Finish by checking the scale: a laboratory brake delivers a few kilowatts, so a result in hundreds of kilowatts means either the 60 is missing or a millimetre was left unconverted.

Definition. A dynamometer is an instrument for measuring force, torque or power. In its simplest, force-measuring form it measures a load directly, either by the elastic extension of a calibrated spring, F = kx, or by comparison against dead weights. In its power-measuring form it determines the torque at a shaft and hence the power from BP = 2πNT/60.

Limitation of the absorption types. Because all the power they measure is converted into heat at a friction or fluid surface, they are limited by how fast that heat can be carried away. The Prony brake, with only the blocks to dissipate it, suits low speeds and small powers; the hydraulic and eddy-current types handle more because water or a large casing surface takes the heat. This is why absorption dynamometers are laboratory instruments.

Exam close: "Hence the classification turns on a single question — whether the dynamometer takes the power away or merely reads it as it passes — and that is why absorption types are confined to test beds, where the power is meant to be wasted, and transmission types are used on drives in service."

Construction. A Prony brake consists of a brake drum keyed to the shaft of the prime mover, with two wooden or metal blocks clamped around it. A lever arm is attached to the blocks and carries a dead weight at its end. The lever arm is measured from the shaft axis.

Working. The blocks are tightened on the drum until the friction between them and the drum absorbs the output of the machine. The friction drags the blocks round in the direction of rotation, and this tendency is balanced by the dead weight on the lever arm. When the arm is in equilibrium, the torque exerted by friction on the drum equals the torque exerted by the weight on the arm, so

T = W x L          and          BP = 2 pi N T / 60

with W the dead weight in newtons, L the lever arm in metres, N the speed in rpm and T the torque in N·m, giving the brake power in watts.

Limitations. All the power absorbed is converted into heat at the friction surface between the blocks and the drum. That heat has no path away except through the blocks and the surrounding air, so the brake can only be used at low speeds and small powers; at higher powers the blocks char, the friction coefficient changes and the reading becomes unreliable. It is also difficult to maintain a steady load, and the blocks wear, so the friction condition drifts during a test.

Exam close: "Hence the Prony brake is the simplest absorption dynamometer, its torque being the dead weight multiplied by the lever arm, and its use is confined to low speeds and small powers because the whole of the power measured is dissipated as heat at the friction surface."

Model answer · 5 marks

A Prony brake has a dead weight of 200 N at a lever arm of 0.6 m and runs at 400 rpm. Find the torque and brake power. A rope brake of drum diameter 0.5 m has a rope of 0.02 m, with W = 300 N and S = 50 N at 300 rpm — find its torque.

Two brakes, and the marks are in the two rope-brake details: the net load and the effective radius.

   PRONY BRAKE                        ROPE BRAKE
   ===========                        ==========

   W = 200 N                          W = 300 N   (dead weight)
   L = 0.6 m                          S =  50 N   (spring balance)
   N = 400 rpm                        D = 0.5 m   (drum dia)
                                      d = 0.02 m  (rope dia)
   T = W x L                          N = 300 rpm
     = 200 x 0.6
     = 120.0 N.m                      net load = W - S
                                                 = 300 - 50 = 250 N

   BP = 2 pi N T / 60
      = 2 x pi x 400 x 120 / 60       effective radius = (D + d)/2
      = 5026.55 W                                        = (0.5 + 0.02)/2
      = 5.03 kW                                          = 0.26 m
                                                          ^
                                      NOT D/2 = 0.25 m ---+  the rope acts
                                                             at its centre line

                                      T = 250 x 0.26 = 65.0 N.m

Part one — Prony brake. Given: W = 200 N, L = 0.6 m, N = 400 rpm. Find: T and BP. Formula: T = WL; BP = 2πNT/60. Solve:

T = 200 x 0.6 = 120.0 N.m

BP = 2 x pi x 400 x 120 / 60
   = 5026.55 W = 5.03 kW

Part two — rope brake. Given: W = 300 N, S = 50 N, D = 0.5 m, d = 0.02 m, N = 300 rpm. Find: T. Formula: T = (W − S)(D + d)/2. Solve:

net load = 300 - 50 = 250 N
effective radius = (0.5 + 0.02)/2 = 0.26 m

T = 250 x 0.26 = 65.0 N.m

Answer: the Prony brake gives a torque of 120 N·m and a brake power of 5.03 kW; the rope brake gives a torque of 65 N·m.

Check: the Prony result substitutes back, 5026.55 × 60/(2π × 400) = 120.0 N·m, and 5.03 kW is the output of a small single-cylinder engine, which is exactly the scale of machine a laboratory brake is used on. For the rope brake, the two details each have a check: using D/2 = 0.25 m instead of 0.26 m would give 62.5 N·m, a 4% understatement, because the rope acts along its own centre line half a rope diameter outside the drum; and using W = 300 N instead of the net 250 N would give 78 N·m, a 20% overstatement, because the spring balance is holding part of the weight. Both errors are arithmetic, but they are the two the question is set to catch.

Exam close: "Hence the Prony brake gives 120 N·m and 5.03 kW directly from the weight and lever arm, while the rope brake requires the net load of 250 N and the effective radius of 0.26 m, giving 65 N·m."
