Sine Bar and Combination Set — Setting an Angle from a Length

A sine bar does something no protractor can: it converts an angle into a length, and a length can be built to microns with slip gauges. That is why it is the standard way of measuring a taper and setting a workpiece for grinding. RGPV asks it in 4 of 11 papers, usually as “construction, use, and how a taper is measured” — and the whole topic is one formula, so the marks are in drawing it correctly.

The one-sentence statement

A sine bar is a hardened steel bar carrying two equal rollers of known
centre distance L, one at each end, mounted on the underside. Placing the
bar on a surface plate with one roller resting on a slip-gauge stack of
height h tilts it through the angle given by

        sin(theta) = h / L

so a required angle is SET by building h = L sin(theta) from slip gauges,
and an unknown angle is MEASURED by reading h and computing
theta = sin^-1 (h / L).

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “The sine bar carries no graduations at all — it is only a triangle. All of its accuracy comes from the slip gauge stack h and the centre distance L, which is why its limit is a one-line statement about those two lengths.”

Where the accuracy comes from

The sine bar itself contributes almost no error, because the two things that matter are known precisely: L is lapped to a known centre distance and h is built from slip gauges accurate to a fraction of a micron. The two real limits are:

  • L must be known exactly. Since sin θ = h/L, an error in L propagates straight into the angle. This is why a sine bar is only as good as its certified centre distance.
  • Small angles are the weak case. Near θ the sine curve is flat, so a change in h produces very little change in the angle; at large angles the same h error produces much more. For this reason sine bars are not recommended for angles above about 45°, where the sensitivity of the sin function to the height error grows.

Uses

  1. To set a workpiece at a required angle for machining or inspection — build h = L sin θ from slip gauges, place the bar, and the top face is at θ.
  2. To measure an unknown angle or taper — tilt the bar until its top face lies flush with the taper surface, measure the height h under the high roller, and compute θ = sin−1(h / L).
  3. To measure the included angle of a taper — the same operation gives half the included angle, which must then be doubled.
  4. To set centres for grinding — a sine bar or sine centre holds the work at the exact half-angle so the wheel cuts the correct taper.
The half-angle trap. When a sine bar is used on a taper, the angle it gives is the half-angle — the inclination of one side. The included angle is twice that. So a taper whose sine gives 11.54° has an included angle of 23.07°. Forgetting to double is a standard lost mark, and it is worth one explicit line in every taper answer.

Combination set

The name is used for two quite different things, and it is worth separating them because a question can ask for either.

MeaningWhat it isWhere it appears
Slip gauge combination setThe 81-piece set of gauge blocks from which any length from 1 to 1000 mm is built by stacking, normally three gauges at a timeFeeding the sine bar; any precise length setting
Combination square setA combination of a steel rule with a square head, a protractor head and a centre head, used for layout and for checking angles and centresWorkshop layout and marking out

In the context of the sine bar, the first meaning is the one that matters: the slip gauge combination set is what supplies h. Recall from topic 02 that a length is built by decomposing it by decimal place and stacking the blocks, with the differential gauge making up the final small residual.

Solved A — setting a required angle

A sine bar has a roller centre distance of 200 mm. What height of slip gauge stack is required to set it at an angle of 30°?

Given: L = 200 mm, θ = 30°. Find: h. Formula: h = L sin θ. Solve:

h = 200 x sin 30 deg
  = 200 x 0.5
  = 100.00 mm

Answer: a slip gauge stack of 100.00 mm is required under the high roller.

Check: substituting back, sin−1(100/200) = sin−1(0.5) = 30°, exactly the required angle. Independent second check on the arithmetic: 30° is one of the standard angles whose sine is exactly 0.5, so the answer must be exactly half of L — and 100.00 is half of 200. If the answer had come out 173.2 mm, the working would have used cos 30° instead of sin 30°, which is the commonest error in this topic and the reason the substitution check is worth doing.

Solved B — measuring an unknown angle

The same 200 mm sine bar is set up on a component, and the slip gauge stack under the high roller measures 70.71 mm. Find the angle of the component.

Given: L = 200 mm, h = 70.71 mm. Find: θ. Formula: θ = sin−1(h / L). Solve:

sin(theta) = 70.71 / 200
           = 0.35355
theta      = sin^-1 (0.35355)
           = 20.70 deg

Answer: the angle of the component is 20.70°.

Check: substituting back, 200 × sin 20.70° = 200 × 0.35355 = 70.71 mm, returning the measured height exactly. Independently, a useful sanity anchor: the same bar at 20° would give 200 × 0.34202 = 68.40 mm and at 21° would give 200 × 0.35837 = 71.67 mm, so a height of 70.71 mm must fall between 20° and 21° — and 20.70° does. Bracketing the answer between two known angles is a fast way to catch a wrong inverse-sine entry.

Solved C — the included angle of a taper

A taper is measured with a sine bar of centre distance 150 mm. With the bar set against one side of the taper, the slip gauge stack under the high roller measures 30.00 mm. Find the half-angle and the included angle of the taper.

The bar measures the inclination of one face, which is the half-angle. The included angle is twice that.

Given: L = 150 mm, h = 30.00 mm. Find: half-angle and included angle. Formula: θ = sin−1(h / L); θincluded = 2θ. Solve:

sin(theta) = 30.00 / 150
           = 0.20000
theta      = sin^-1 (0.20000)
           = 11.54 deg          <- HALF angle

included   = 2 x 11.54
           = 23.07 deg

Answer: half-angle 11.54°, included angle 23.07°.

Check: substituting back, 150 × sin 11.54° = 150 × 0.20000 = 30.00 mm, returning the measured height. Second check on the doubling: the included angle must be larger than the half-angle and less than twice 45°, and 23.07° satisfies both. Third check by bracketing: sin 11° = 0.19081 and sin 12° = 0.20791, so a sine of 0.20000 lies between 11° and 12°, and 11.54° does. If the working had stopped at 11.54° and called that the included angle, the answer would be wrong by a factor of two — the single most common lost mark on a taper question.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • L is the distance between the roller centres, not the length of the bar and not the distance between the ends. Under-by-100 or over-by-100 readings come from measuring the wrong span.
  • Sine, not cosine. h is the opposite side, so sin θ = h/L. The cos form belongs to the adjacent side and is the standard wrong answer.
  • Double it for a taper. The bar gives the half-angle; the included angle is twice that.
  • Work in degrees. A calculator left in radian mode returns 0.52 instead of 30°. State the unit on every angle.
  • h is built from slip gauges, so it must be buildable. A required 100.00 mm stack is valid; a required 100.0001 mm stack is beyond what a standard set resolves.
  • Sine bars are limited to about 45°. Above that the sine function is too sensitive to the h error, so an answer of 70° from a sine bar is physically suspicious.
  • Do not confuse the two combination sets. The slip gauge set builds h; the combination square set is layout equipment.
  • Keep full precision in h. 70.71 mm is four significant figures; rounding it to 71 changes the angle by about 0.1°.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “A sine bar is a precision instrument used for setting and measuring angles, consisting of a hardened steel bar with two equal rollers of known centre distance fixed on its underside.”
  • Principle line — “The bar works on the sine principle, so that sin θ = h / L, where h is the height of the slip gauge stack under the high roller and L is the distance between the roller centres.”
  • Setting line — “To set a given angle the slip gauge stack is built to height L sin θ and placed under one roller, which tilts the top face to that angle.”
  • Measuring line — “To measure an unknown angle the bar is tilted until its top face is flush with the surface, the height is measured, and the angle is obtained as sin−1(h / L).”
  • Limitation line — “The accuracy of the sine bar depends on the accuracy of the centre distance L and of the slip gauge stack h, and it is not recommended for angles greater than about 45°.”
  • Closing line — “Hence the sine bar converts an angle into a length, and since a length can be built from slip gauges to a fraction of a micron, the angle can be set and measured to a corresponding accuracy.”

Sine Bar and Combination Set — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

A sine bar is a precision instrument for setting and measuring angles,
consisting of a hardened steel bar with two equal rollers of known centre
distance L fixed on its underside. It works on the sine principle:

        sin(theta) = h / L

where h is the height of the slip gauge stack placed under the high roller.

SET an angle   :  build h = L sin(theta) from slip gauges
MEASURE an angle: read h, then theta = sin^-1 (h / L)

For a TAPER, the angle obtained is the HALF-ANGLE; the included angle is
TWICE it.

The accuracy depends on L and h, not on the bar. Not recommended above
about 45 degrees, where the sine function loses sensitivity.

Derivation in four steps

1. Geometry    -> the bar, the surface plate and the stack form a right
                  triangle; L is the hypotenuse, h the opposite side
2. Relation    -> sin(theta) = opposite / hypotenuse = h / L
3. Rearranged  -> h = L sin(theta)      (to SET an angle)
                  theta = sin^-1(h/L)   (to MEASURE an angle)
4. Taper       -> theta is the HALF angle; included angle = 2 x theta

Definitions to reproduce

  • Sine bar — a hardened steel bar with two equal rollers of known centre distance on its underside, used to set and measure angles on the sine principle.
  • Centre distance L — the distance between the centres of the two rollers; the hypotenuse of the triangle and the quantity the instrument's accuracy depends on.
  • Slip gauge stack h — the height built from slip gauges under the high roller to tilt the bar to a required angle.
  • Sine principle — the relation sin θ = h / L, which converts an angle into a length.
  • Half-angle — the angle of inclination of one face of a taper, which is what a sine bar measures when set against it.
  • Included angle — the total angle between the two faces of a taper, equal to twice the half-angle.
  • Slip gauge combination set — the 81-piece set of gauge blocks from which any length from 1 to 1000 mm is built by stacking, normally three gauges at a time.
  • Combination square set — a steel rule with square, protractor and centre heads, used for layout and for checking angles, a different instrument from the above.

Numeral shortcut

One relation, three rearrangements. sin θ = h/L. To set an angle: h = L sin θ. To measure an angle: θ = sin−1(h/L). For a taper: double the result. Anchor values worth carrying — sin 30° = 0.5 so h is half of L, and sin 45° = 0.707 so h is 0.707 of L. Always finish by substituting the answer back so that L sin θ returns h, and check the angle lies in the bracket the neighbouring angles give.

Explain the construction and working of a sine bar. How is it used to measure a taper?

Construction. A sine bar consists of a hardened steel bar rectangular in section, whose top and bottom faces are ground and lapped flat and parallel. Two equal rollers, ground and lapped to the same diameter, are fixed on the underside near the two ends. The distance between the centres of these rollers is the centre distance L, and it is known exactly — standard bars have centre distances of 100, 200, 250 or 300 mm. The bar carries no graduations whatever.

Principle and working. The bar rests on a surface plate at one roller and on a stack of slip gauges at the other. The bar, the plate and the stack therefore form a right-angled triangle in which the centre distance L is the hypotenuse, the stack height h is the side opposite the angle of tilt, and the angle θ is the inclination of the bar. Hence

sin(theta) = h / L

  to SET an angle    :  h = L sin(theta)
  to MEASURE an angle:  theta = sin^-1 (h / L)

Because a length can be built from slip gauges to a fraction of a micron, an angle can be set to a correspondingly fine accuracy — which is the whole reason the instrument exists.

Measuring a taper. The bar is set with its top face in contact with one face of the taper, and the height h under the high roller is measured. The angle obtained from θ = sin−1(h / L) is the inclination of that one face, that is the half-angle of the taper. The included angle of the taper is twice this value. For example, a stack of 30.00 mm under a 150 mm bar gives sin θ = 0.2000, so θ = 11.54° and the included angle is 23.07°.

Exam close: "Hence the sine bar converts an angle into a length, and since the slip gauge stack can be built to a fraction of a micron the angle can be set and measured to a corresponding accuracy — the bar itself contributing nothing, since it carries no graduations and its accuracy rests entirely on the centre distance L."

Model answer · 5 marks

What are the uses and limitations of a sine bar?

Four uses, three limitations, and every limitation carries its reason. The 45 degree sensitivity limit is the one that separates a complete answer.

   USES                              LIMITATIONS
   ----                              -----------
   set a workpiece                   accuracy depends on L
   at a required angle               (a certified centre distance)
        |                                    |
   measure an unknown                accuracy depends on the
   angle or taper                    slip gauge stack h
        |                                    |
   measure the included              sin curve flattens above
   angle of a taper                  45 deg -> less sensitive
        |                                    |
   set centres for grinding          small angles are the
   (sine centres)                    easy case, large ones
                                     are not

        THE SINE CURVE - why 45 degrees is the limit

   sin(th) |
    1.00 -|                                 ....--*  <- flat here:
          |                          ...--''          large change in h
    0.71 -|              ....--*''                    for little change
          |      ...--''   ^                         in angle
    0.50 -|  ..--*        |
          | *           45 deg
          *  ^
          |  small angles: steep part of the curve,
          |  so h reads very sensitively
    0.00 -+-----------------------------------------> th
         0     30     45     60     90

Uses.

  • To set a workpiece at a required angle for machining or inspection: the stack is built to h = L sin θ and placed under the high roller, which tilts the top face to that angle.
  • To measure an unknown angle or taper: the bar is tilted until its top face is flush with the surface, h is measured, and θ = sin−1(h/L).
  • To measure the included angle of a taper: the same operation gives the half-angle, which is doubled.
  • To set centres for grinding: a sine bar or sine centre holds the work at the exact half-angle so that the grinding wheel cuts the correct taper.

Limitations.

  • It depends on L. Since sin θ = h/L, any error in the centre distance propagates directly into the angle, so the bar is only as good as its certified centre distance.
  • It depends on h. The slip gauge stack must itself be built to the required accuracy; the instrument's precision is bounded by the gauge blocks.
  • It is not recommended above about 45°. As the angle grows, the sine function flattens, so a given error in h produces a progressively larger error in the angle. Below 45° the curve is steep and the height reads very sensitively; above it the sensitivity is lost.

Exam close: "Hence the sine bar is accurate because it converts an angle into a length rather than reading the angle directly, and its limits are the accuracies of the centre distance and the stack together with the loss of sensitivity beyond about 45 degrees."

Model answer · 5 marks

A sine bar of centre distance 200 mm reads a slip gauge stack of 70.71 mm. Find the angle. What stack would be needed to set it at 30°?

Both directions through the same relation. Part one takes the inverse sine; part two takes the forward sine. Marks are per step, so write the formula before substituting.

   MEASURE:  h known, theta wanted        SET:  theta known, h wanted

        L = 200 mm                            L = 200 mm
        h = 70.71 mm                          theta = 30 deg
             \                                     \
              \                                     \
        sin(th) = h / L                       h = L sin(th)
               = 70.71 / 200                     = 200 x sin 30
               = 0.35355                          = 200 x 0.5
                                                 = 100.00 mm
        th = sin^-1 (0.35355)
           = 20.70 deg

   Bracket check: 20 deg -> 68.40 mm     Anchor: sin 30 = 0.5 exactly,
                  21 deg -> 71.67 mm             so h is half of L
                  70.71 lies between     -> 100.00 is half of 200
                  -> answer is between
                     20 and 21 deg

Part one. Given: L = 200 mm, h = 70.71 mm. Find: θ. Formula: θ = sin−1(h/L). Solve:

sin(theta) = 70.71 / 200 = 0.35355
theta      = sin^-1 (0.35355) = 20.70 deg

Part two. Given: L = 200 mm, θ = 30°. Find: h. Formula: h = L sin θ. Solve:

h = 200 x sin 30 deg
  = 200 x 0.5
  = 100.00 mm

Answer: the component angle is 20.70°; to set the bar at 30° a slip gauge stack of 100.00 mm is required.

Check: both parts substitute back. For part one, 200 × sin 20.70° = 200 × 0.35355 = 70.71 mm, recovering the measured height. For part two, sin−1(100/200) = 30°, recovering the required angle. Bracket check on part one: the same bar gives 200 × sin 20° = 68.40 mm and 200 × sin 21° = 71.67 mm, so a height of 70.71 mm must correspond to an angle between 20° and 21° — and 20.70° does. Note also that 30° is a standard angle whose sine is exactly 0.5, so part two must give exactly half of L; a result other than 100.00 mm would indicate that the cosine had been used.

Exam close: "Hence the same relation is used in both directions — forward to set a required angle by building a height, and inverse to measure an unknown angle from a measured height — and the angle is always a value of sin−1(h/L) expressed in degrees."
