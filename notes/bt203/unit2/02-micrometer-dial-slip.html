Micrometer, Dial Gauge and Slip Gauge — The Instruments That Read 0.01 mm

This is the single most-repeated question in BT-203: “Explain the working of a micrometer” appears in 6 of 11 papers — and RGPV asks it as a labelled sketch with its working, least count and range. All three instruments here resolve 0.01 mm, which is why they are examined as a set. Learn the screw’s arithmetic properly and the sketch writes itself; get it wrong and every part of the answer collapses.

The one-sentence statement

A micrometer (micrometer screw gauge) measures a length by converting the
rotation of a screw of 0.5 mm pitch, divided into 50 divisions, into a
distance on the graduated sleeve and the thimble. Least count = 0.5 / 50
= 0.01 mm; range 0 to 25 mm (or 0 to 50 mm or 0 to 100 mm). The dial
gauge reads the same 0.01 mm but as a comparator; a slip gauge builds a
known length by stacking gauges.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “All three read 0.01 mm, and they differ in what they compare against — a screw thread, a reference setting, or a standard of length.”

Why the least count is 0.01 mm

This is the number the examiner is checking. Do not memorise it — derive it, in two lines.

Pitch of the screw      p = 0.5 mm   (given, by the screw standard)
Number of divisions     N = 50      (marked on the thimble)
Least count   LC = p / N = 0.5 / 50 = 0.01 mm   = 10 micron

Pitch 0.5 mm, not 1 mm — the fact everyone gets wrong

A standard metric micrometer screw has a pitch of 0.5 mm, not 1 mm. This is because 0.5 mm is exactly divisible by both the 50-division thimble (giving 0.01 mm) and the 100-division thimble (giving 0.005 mm), so either thimble gives an exact decimal. If a question says the pitch is 1 mm, use the 1 mm it gives you — but the default, assumed when nothing is stated, is 0.5 mm.

So: LC = 0.5 / 50 = 0.01 mm, and range = the spindle length — a 25 mm spindle gives a 0–25 mm instrument, a 50 mm spindle gives 0–50 mm. The number 10 x spindle also appears, but it is the total travel, not the range.

Reading a micrometer — three rules

  1. Main scale reading. Read the last graduation on the sleeve that is visible to the left of the thimble edge. The sleeve is graduated in 0.5 mm steps, so this contributes 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5…
  2. Thimble reading (circular scale). The thimble divisions are numbered 0, 5, 10…50 (0 and 50 are the same line). Read the division that coincides with the sleeve datum line, i.e. the CS.
  3. Add them. Measured value = main scale reading + CS. Then apply any zero correction from the box.

Zero error and zero correction

ConditionZero errorCorrectionApplied how
Jaws closed and the zero lines coincidezeronone—
Jaws closed and the zero line is below the datum (gap between thimble zero and datum)negativepositivemeasured value + correction
Jaws closed and the zero line is above the datumpositivenegativemeasured value − correction
The rule, in one line

Correction is always the opposite sign to the error, and you always add the correction. So a micrometer that reads high with the jaws closed (positive zero error) has a negative correction, which you subtract. Memorise the paired signs, not the story: the exam asks “what is the correction” and wants a signed number.

Dial gauge — a comparator, not a ruler

The dial gauge has the same 0.01 mm least count, but a completely different job. It has no spindle to set against an absolute datum; instead the plunger is always pressed against something, and you set the dial to zero on the master and then read how far the work deviates from it.

Why RGPV pairs them. Both resolve 0.01 mm, which is the detail the question is usually testing — candidates routinely give two different least counts for the pair. Slip gauge — length built by stacking

A slip gauge (or gauge block) is a rectangular block of hardened steel, lapped flat on two opposite faces to an accuracy of a fraction of a micron. Individual gauges have standard sizes (more below), and any required length is built by stacking them.

TermMeaning
Gauge numberThe nominal length of one gauge, so it can be identified without measuring it
Gauge series A, B, CDifferent sets of standard sizes. Series A is a set of decimal gauges for any decimal length; Series B gives binary and digit combinations; Series C is a smaller supplementary set
Differential gaugeA thinner block so you can remove length as well as add it
Roller gaugeA long slim block used for slenderness and for guarding large gauges
 
     BUILD A LENGTH BY STACKING
     ================================================
      REQUIRED: 35.20 mm

      35 mm      (one gauge from the decimal set)
       0.2 mm    (one small gauge)
       -----
      35.20 mm   = 35 + 0.2      -> the stack IS the length

      The gauges are wrung together (thin film of oil,
      or a light sliding motion). A wrung stack of N
      gauges carries N-1 separate interfaces, and its
      length is exactly the sum of the individuals.

     MEASURE IT BACK with the two-piece method
     ================================================
     1. Measure the 35 mm gauge ALONE with the
        micrometer  -> x
     2. Measure the WHOLE stack (35 + 0.2) -> y
     3. size of the 0.2 gauge = y - x
        (this is how a worn or suspect small gauge
         is actually certified)

     Differential gauge = the one you subtract
     to come DOWN to size, e.g. 35.20 - 0.05 = 35.15
The two-piece method. This is how a gauge block is certified and it is the single most likely short question on slip gauges: measure the large gauge alone, measure the stack, subtract to find the size of the small gauge. Writing that sentence with a sketch of a stack is a full answer.

Combination set

A combination set is the standard group of gauges with which any length from 1 to 1000 mm can be built. It contains 81 pieces in nine groups — an 81-piece set carries gauges for 1.000–9.000 in 0.001 steps, 10.00–100.0 in 0.01 steps, and so on up to 100–1000 in 0.1 steps, plus the differential and roller gauges. You always select three gauges to build a length.

Solved A — reading a micrometer with a zero error

A 0–25 mm micrometer has a pitch of 0.5 mm and 50 thimble divisions. With the jaws closed the thimble zero lies 0.02 mm above the sleeve datum line. A shaft is measured and the observation is: main scale reading 2.5 mm, thimble division 47 coinciding with the datum. Find the least count, the range, the zero error, and the true diameter.

Given: p = 0.5 mm, N = 50, spindle dia 25 mm; zero 0.02 mm above datum; MSR = 2.5 mm, CS = 47. Find: LC, range, zero error and its sign, true diameter. Formula: LC = p/N; range = d; TV = MSR + CS − z with the sign of z read from the statement. Solve:

LC    = 0.5 / 50 = 0.01 mm

range = 0 to 25 mm
        (the instrument opens to the spindle diameter 25 mm;
         total travel over many turns = 10 x 25 = 250 mm,
         but the RANGE is only what it can read in one setup)

zero lies ABOVE the datum -> positive zero error
z = +0.02 mm

MV = 2.5 + 0.47 = 2.97 mm
TV = 2.97 - 0.02 = 2.95 mm

Answer: least count 0.01 mm, range 0–25 mm, zero error +0.02 mm (correction −0.02 mm), true diameter 2.95 mm.

Check: adding the correction back recovers the measured value exactly, 2.95 + 0.02 = 2.97 mm, so the sign of the correction is right — an instrument reading high with its jaws closed needs its reading reduced. Independent second check on the main scale: the sleeve is graduated in 0.5 mm steps and the last visible mark was 2.5 mm, so the value must lie between 2.50 and 2.99 mm. 2.95 sits inside that band; had the thimble read, say, 57 the sum would have fallen outside it, which flags a misread. Note also that 2.95 is not a multiple of 0.01 from 2.97 by accident — the 0.02 correction is an exact two divisions, so the final figure is legitimately a two-decimal millimetre value. On the range: 25 mm is both the spindle diameter and the instrument's range, because a spindle that long can open to its own diameter and no further. The figure 250 mm is the total travel, the distance the spindle would move through many turns, and writing it as the range is the classic error — which is why it is listed as a trap below.

Solved B — least count and range of a different spindle

A micrometer has a screw of pitch 0.5 mm, 100 divisions on the thimble and a spindle of diameter 50 mm. Find its least count, its range, and the reading it gives when the main scale shows 1.5 mm and thimble division 23 coincides with the datum, assuming no zero error.

Given: p = 0.5 mm, N = 100, d = 50 mm; MSR = 1.5 mm, CS = 23. Find: LC, range, reading. Formula: LC = p/N; range = d; MV = MSR + CS. Solve:

LC = 0.5 / 100 = 0.005 mm        <- finer than the standard 50-division
                                  thimble, because N is larger

range = 0 to 50 mm
        (spindle is 50 mm long, so it opens to its own diameter;
         total travel = 10 x 50 = 500 mm over many turns)

MV = 1.5 + 0.23 = 1.73 mm

Answer: least count 0.005 mm, range 0–50 mm, reading 1.73 mm.

Check: the least count comes out as an exact decimal because 0.5 divides evenly by 100 — that is precisely why the screw pitch is 0.5 mm rather than 1 mm, and it is the reason the check on the pitch is worth remembering. On the range: a 50 mm spindle cannot open past 50 mm, so the instrument reads from 0 to 50 mm even though the spindle would travel 500 mm through many turns; this distinction (range versus total travel) is worth one line in the answer. On the reading: 1.73 mm is a multiple of 0.005 — it is 346 divisions of the thimble — as it must be for a 0.005 mm instrument. A value like 1.735 would not be readable on this instrument.

Solved C — building a length from slip gauges

Construct a length of 83.425 mm from an 81-piece combination set, stating the three gauges used. Then state how you would verify the size of the smallest gauge used.

Given: required length 83.425 mm; standard combination set. Find: the three gauges. Formula: decimal decomposition — thousands, then hundreds/tens/units, then the decimal part; the last component comes from the differential. Solve:

83.425 mm  =  80 mm  +  3 mm  +  0.425 mm
              (gauge)   (gauge)    (differential)

the 0.425 must be reached in smaller steps:
  0.425 = 0.4 + 0.025        -> 4th group gives 0.400
  0.025 -> 0.02 + 0.005     -> 5th group gives 0.020
   the remainder 0.005       -> differential gauge

so the stack is three or four gauges selected across groups:
   80.000 (group 3) + 3.000 (group 2) + 0.425 assembled from the
   small gauges / differential

Answer: the length is built by stacking, never by cutting: decompose the number by decimal place — 83.000 from the large gauges, 0.400 and 0.020 from the smaller groups, and the final 0.005 from the differential gauge. The exact selection depends on which gauges the set contains; the method — split by decimal place, three or four gauges, finish with the differential — is what earns the mark.

Check: the components must sum exactly to the target: 83.000 + 0.400 + 0.020 + 0.005 = 83.425 mm. Independent second check: a combination set can build any length in its range, so if your chosen pieces do not sum to the target the selection is wrong, not the method. Third check on the digit count: 83.425 has three whole-number digits and three decimal digits, so you need one gauge per decimal place plus a differential — four pieces, which matches.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Pitch is 0.5 mm, not 1 mm. LC = 0.5/50 = 0.01 mm. Dividing 1 by 50 gives 0.02 mm, which is the most common wrong answer on this topic.
  • Range is the spindle length, not ten times it. A 25 mm spindle gives 0–25 mm. The figure 250 mm is the total travel, a different quantity entirely.
  • Add the two scale readings; never subtract them. Main scale gives the whole 0.5 mm steps, thimble gives the fraction below that step.
  • Read the last division visible to the left of the thimble edge — not the one the edge is touching, and not the next one.
  • Zero error sign: above the datum is positive, below is negative. And the correction always carries the opposite sign.
  • Do not confuse the micrometer’s 0–25 mm range with its 250 mm total travel. The range is what it can measure in one setup.
  • Dial gauge reads a deviation, not a size. It must be set to zero against a master; without that setting it has no absolute meaning.
  • Slip gauges are stacked, not combined by subtraction of size. The sum of the pieces is the length; the differential is the one piece you subtract.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “A micrometer is a screw-gauge instrument that converts the rotation of a screw of 0.5 mm pitch divided into 50 circumferential divisions into a measurable distance, and it is used to measure small internal or external dimensions to an accuracy of 0.01 mm.”
  • Least-count line — “The least count is pitch divided by the number of divisions, that is 0.5/50 = 0.01 mm, and the range equals the length of the spindle.”
  • Method line — “The measurement is the sum of the main scale reading on the sleeve and the circular scale reading on the thimble, corrected for any zero error.”
  • Dial gauge line — “The dial gauge has the same 0.01 mm least count but is a comparator: it is set to zero against a master and reads only the deviation of the work from that master.”
  • Slip gauge line — “A slip gauge is a hardened block lapped to a standard length; any required length is built by wringing several gauges together, and the size of a single small gauge is verified by measuring it alone and then measuring the stack, subtracting one from the other.”
  • Closing line — “Hence all three instruments resolve 0.01 mm, and they are distinguished by the reference they are read against — a screw thread, a set master, or a stacked standard.”

  3. Recurring short question. “What is a slip gauge? How is a length formed from it?” → Definition; series A, B, C; stacking and wringing; the differential gauge; the two-piece method of verifying a gauge.

Definitions to reproduce

  • Micrometer — an instrument measuring small dimensions by converting screw rotation into linear displacement, using a 0.5 mm pitch screw with a 50-division thimble.
  • Pitch — the axial distance the spindle advances in one full turn of the screw; 0.5 mm in a standard metric micrometer.
  • Least count — the smallest change the instrument can measure; here pitch divided by thimble divisions, 0.01 mm.
  • Zero error — the reading obtained with the jaws closed against each other when it should be zero; positive when the zero lies above the datum line.
  • Zero correction — the negative of the zero error; always applied, with the sign opposite to the error.
  • Dial gauge — a comparator of 0.01 mm least count, zeroed against a master, reading the deviation of the work from that master.
  • Slip gauge or gauge block — a hardened steel block lapped flat on two opposite faces to a highly accurate standard length.
  • Combination set — the 81-piece group of gauges from which any length from 1 to 1000 mm can be built, selecting three gauges.
  • Differential gauge — the thinnest gauge, used to bring a stack down in size rather than up.
  • Roller gauge — a long slim gauge used for slenderness and to guard the smaller gauges.

Numeral shortcut

Three moves. Least count: pitch divided by divisions — 0.5/50 = 0.01. Range: the spindle length. Reading: add the sleeve reading to the thimble reading, then apply the zero correction with the sign opposite to the zero error. Always finish by writing the unit and checking the value is a multiple of 0.01 mm — an answer that is not a multiple of the least count has been misread.

Explain the working of a micrometer with a neat sketch. State its least count and range.

Sketch, then working, then the two numbers with their derivations. The pitch of 0.5 mm is the fact the whole answer rests on, so show it.

Construction. A micrometer consists of a U-shaped frame, an anvil which is the fixed measuring face, a spindle which is the moving face, a graduated sleeve bearing the main scale and the datum line, and a thimble bearing 50 circumferential divisions on its bevelled edge. A ratchet stop at the end of the spindle provides a constant measuring pressure.

Working. The work is held between the anvil and the spindle. The spindle is carried by a screw of 0.5 mm pitch, so one complete turn moves it 0.5 mm axially — and the axial movement is what is read, not the rotation. The sleeve's horizontal main scale is graduated in steps of 0.5 mm and records the thimble-edge position; the thimble is divided into 50 equal parts, so each division is 0.5/50 = 0.01 mm. The reading is the sum of the last main scale division visible to the left of the thimble edge and the thimble division coinciding with the sleeve datum line. The ratchet stop is operated so that the spindle is applied to the work at a constant pressure; a micrometer fitted with a ratchet must therefore not be held by turning the thimble directly.

QuantityDerivationValue
Least countpitch / thimble divisions = 0.5 / 500.01 mm
Range (25 mm spindle)equal to the spindle length; 10 x 25 = 250 mm is the total travel, not the range0 to 25 mm

Zero error. With the jaws closed the zero of the thimble should coincide with the sleeve datum line. If it lies above the datum the zero error is positive and the correction is negative; if it lies below, the reverse. The correction always carries the sign opposite to the error and is applied to every reading.

Exam close: "Hence a micrometer reads to 0.01 mm by converting screw rotation into axial motion, the pitch of 0.5 mm divided into 50 thimble divisions giving the least count, and its range being the length of the spindle — which is why a 0–25 mm instrument is the commonest form."

Model answer · 5 marks

What is a dial gauge? Explain its working and state its applications.

One sentence carries the whole distinction: it is a comparator. Everything after that is working detail and applications that are genuinely checking operations.

   MICROMETER: absolute reading            DIAL GAUGE: comparator

        |ANVIL|                              MASTER (gauge block)
        |    |  <- work held between         |#######|  set to zero
        |    |     the two faces              |#######|
        +----+                               ++-----+
     sleeve 2.5                              \  |  /
     thimble 0.47                            \ | /   plunger presses on
     ----------                               \|/    the work
       = 2.97 mm                                v
                                          WORK (shaft)
     ONE number, printed on the instrument  if dial reads +0.03,
     straight off the scale                  the work is 0.03 BIGGER
                                             than the master;
                                             if it reads -0.02, smaller.
                                             The dial itself never
                                             says what the size is.

Definition. A dial gauge is a comparator of 0.01 mm least count, used to check the deviation of a work from a standard rather than to measure its size directly.

Working. Before use, the dial is set to zero against a master — a gauge block, a setting standard, or a known good component. The plunger is then pressed against the work by a lever, the spindle compresses a spring, and the dial indicates the displacement from the setting. A reading of +0.03 mm means the work is 0.03 mm larger than the master; a reading of −0.02 mm means it is smaller. Because the reference is set by the operator, the dial alone never indicates an absolute dimension.

Applications. Checking the flatness of a surface; checking the eccentricity and run-out of a shaft; comparing a hole against its plug gauge; checking the clearance between a shaft and its bearing; and checking the parallelism of a machined face. In every case it answers “is this within limits” rather than “what exactly is it”.

Exam close: "Hence the dial gauge shares the micrometer's 0.01 mm least count but differs entirely in reference — the micrometer is zeroed by closing its jaws and reads an absolute size, while the dial gauge is zeroed against a master and reads only a deviation, which is what makes it the faster instrument for inspection."

Model answer · 5 marks

What is a slip gauge? How is a required length formed from it, and how is a single gauge verified?

Three parts, and the third is the one that gets missed. The two-piece method is a complete answer in a sentence once it is named and stated.

     BUILD A LENGTH BY STACKING
     ================================================
      REQUIRED: 35.20 mm

      35 mm      (one gauge from the decimal set)
       0.2 mm    (one small gauge)
       -----
      35.20 mm   = 35 + 0.2      -> the stack IS the length

      The gauges are wrung together (thin film of oil,
      or a light sliding motion). A wrung stack of N
      gauges carries N-1 separate interfaces, and its
      length is exactly the sum of the individuals.

     MEASURE IT BACK with the two-piece method
     ================================================
     1. Measure the 35 mm gauge ALONE with the
        micrometer  -> x
     2. Measure the WHOLE stack (35 + 0.2) -> y
     3. size of the 0.2 gauge = y - x
        (this is how a worn or suspect small gauge
         is actually certified)

     Differential gauge = the one you subtract
     to come DOWN to size, e.g. 35.20 - 0.05 = 35.15

Definition. A slip gauge, or gauge block, is a rectangular block of hardened steel lapped flat on two opposite faces to an accuracy of a fraction of a micron. Each gauge has one fixed, standardised length, identified by its gauge number. Gauges are supplied in series: series A gives decimal combinations, series B binary and digit combinations, and series C is a smaller supplementary set. A differential gauge is a thinner block used to bring a stack down in size, and a roller gauge is a long slim block used for slenderness and to guard the smaller gauges.

Forming a length. A required length is built by stacking the appropriate gauges and wringing them together — a thin film of oil or a light sliding motion makes them adhere, so the stack is a single rigid unit. In a standard 81-piece combination set any length from 1 to 1000 mm can be built, normally by selecting three gauges. The number is decomposed by decimal place: the whole-millimetre part from the large gauges, then each successive decimal place from a smaller group, finishing with the differential gauge to correct the final residual.

Verifying a single gauge. By the two-piece method. Measure the large gauge alone with the micrometer, obtaining x. Measure the whole stack, obtaining y. Then the size of the small gauge is y − x. This is how a gauge that is too small to be measured directly, or one suspected of wear, is certified.

Exam close: "Hence a slip gauge has no adjustable or sliding element at all — it is a fixed standard — and a required length is obtained only by stacking several of them, with the differential gauge providing the final subtraction."
