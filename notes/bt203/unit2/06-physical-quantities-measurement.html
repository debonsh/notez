Lesson · BT203 Unit 2 — Measurement & Production

Measurement of Temperature, Pressure, Velocity, Flow, Force and Torque

The MST-1 syllabus page lists six quantities in one line, and RGPV asks about them as “methods of measurement — explain any one”. Temperature alone appears in 4 of 11 papers. So this topic is a matched list: one quantity, one instrument, one principle. Learn the pairing and the question becomes a lookup table rather than a memory test.

The one-sentence statement

Each physical quantity has a class of instrument that works on one physical
principle:

  temperature -> expansion (thermometer) or emf (thermocouple) or radiation (pyrometer)
  pressure    -> a liquid column (manometer) or elastic deflection (Bourdon gauge)
  velocity    -> pitot tube, from the difference of total and static pressure
  flow        -> variable head (venturi and orifice), or variable area (rotameter)
  force       -> elastic deformation (spring balance), or strain (strain gauge)
  torque      -> force at a measured radius (dynamometer), or shaft twist

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Nothing here is memorised — every one of these instruments converts the quantity into something easier to measure: a length, a deflection, or a voltage. Say what it converts the quantity into, and the principle states itself.”

Temperature — the quantity with the most instruments

RangeInstrumentWhy this one here
Below about 600°CLiquid-in-glass thermometerSimple and direct, but the liquid boils or freezes outside the range, and the glass softens at high temperature
About −200 to 1200°CThermocoupleWide range, rugged, and the junction can be placed remotely and read electrically
About −200 to 600°CResistance thermometerMost accurate in its range, because resistance changes reproducibly with temperature
Above about 1200°COptical or radiation pyrometerNon-contact — it reads the radiation from the body, so it neither melts nor cools what it measures
The pyrometer's one job. A pyrometer exists for exactly one reason: it does not touch the object. Above about 1200°C no contact instrument survives, and contact would also draw heat from the body being measured. If a question asks how the temperature of a furnace or molten metal is measured, the pyrometer is the answer and non-contact is the reason.

Pressure, velocity and flow — the three that share a column of fluid

All three reduce to the same idea: a pressure difference is balanced against a column of liquid, and the height is read.

InstrumentWhat it readsRelation
Piezometer tubeGauge pressure only, for liquidsp = ρgh
U-tube manometerDifference of pressure between two pointsΔp = (ρm − ρf) g h
Differential manometerDifference across a device such as an orificeΔp = ρgh × (Sm − Sf)
Pitot tubeVelocity at a point in the flowv = √(2gh), from total minus static head
Venturi / orifice meterDischarge through a pipeQ = CdA√(2gh), where h is the head difference
RotameterDischarge, read directly off a scaleVariable area — no calculation needed
Venturi against orifice — the standard comparison

Both measure discharge by throttling the flow, and RGPV contrasts them. The difference is the shape of the throat:

  • Orifice meter — a flat plate with a sharp-edged hole. Cheap, simple, compact, and can be dropped into an existing flange. But the sudden contraction and sudden expansion cause a large permanent pressure loss, because the energy in the eddies is lost.
  • Venturi meter — a gradual conical contraction, a cylindrical throat, then a gradual conical expansion. Expensive and bulky, but the streamline flow means the pressure largely recovers downstream, so the permanent loss is small.

So the choice is cost and space against running cost: the orifice is cheap to install and wasteful to run, the venturi is costly to install and efficient to run.

Solved A — pressure from a manometer column

A U-tube manometer containing mercury shows a difference of 250 mm between its two limbs. Find the pressure difference, taking the density of mercury as 13600 kg/m3.

Given: h = 250 mm, ρ = 13600 kg/m3, g = 9.81 m/s2. Find: Δp. Formula: Δp = ρgh. Solve:

h = 250 mm = 0.250 m        (convert to metres FIRST)

p = 13600 x 9.81 x 0.250
  = 33354 Pa
  = 33.35 kPa

Answer: the pressure difference is 33.35 kPa.

Check: substituting back, 33354/(13600 × 9.81) = 0.250 m, recovering the stated head. Independent magnitude check by a rule worth carrying: 1 mm of mercury is about 133 Pa, so 250 mm is 250 × 133 = 33250 Pa = 33.25 kPa — within 0.3% of the exact value, which confirms both the arithmetic and the conversion. Had the 250 been left in millimetres, the answer would have come out 1000 times too large at 33.4 MPa, a pressure at which the manometer itself would have burst.

Solved B — discharge through a venturi meter

A venturi meter with a throat diameter of 50 mm has a coefficient of discharge of 0.62. The manometer across it shows a head difference of 0.5 m of water. Find the discharge.

Given: d = 50 mm, Cd = 0.62, h = 0.5 m. Find: Q. Formula: Q = CdA√(2gh) with A = πd2/4. Solve:

d = 50 mm = 0.050 m         (convert to metres FIRST)

A = pi x (0.050)^2 / 4
  = 0.0019635 m2

sqrt(2gh) = sqrt(2 x 9.81 x 0.5)
          = sqrt(9.81) = 3.132 m/s

Q = 0.62 x 0.0019635 x 3.132
  = 0.003813 m3/s
  = 3.813 litres per second

Answer: the discharge is 0.00381 m3/s, that is 3.81 litres per second.

Check: substituting back, 0.003813/(0.62 × 0.0019635) = 3.132 m/s = √(2gh), recovering the assumed velocity. Independent sense check on magnitude: a 50 mm throat with a 0.5 m head is a small pipe flowing briskly, so a few litres per second is the right order — and multiplying by 1000 converts m3/s to litres/s, which gives 3.81. Had the diameter been used in millimetres, A would have come out 106 times too large and the discharge absurdly so.

Solved C — torque and a temperature conversion

A dynamometer measures a force of 200 N at a radius of 150 mm from the shaft axis. Find the torque. The same instrument reports the lubricating oil at 60°C — state its value on the Fahrenheit scale, and name the instrument you would use for a furnace at 1400°C.

Given: F = 200 N, r = 150 mm; T = 60°C; a furnace at 1400°C. Find: torque, the Fahrenheit reading, and the instrument. Formula: T = Fr with r in metres; °F = (9/5)°C + 32. Solve:

r = 150 mm = 0.150 m

T = 200 x 0.150
  = 30.00 N.m

degF = (9/5) x 60 + 32
     = 108 + 32 = 140 degF

1400 degC  ->  no contact instrument survives;
               use an OPTICAL (RADIATION) PYROMETER, which is
               non-contact and reads the emitted radiation

Answer: torque 30.00 N·m; 60°C is 140°F; a 1400°C furnace requires an optical or radiation pyrometer.

Check: on the torque, 30.00/0.150 = 200 N, recovering the stated force, and 30 N·m is the scale of a hand tool such as a torque wrench, not of a shaft — which is sensible for a bench dynamometer. Had the radius been left as 150 mm, the torque would have come out 30000 N·m, which is turbine scale and three orders out. On the conversion, 60°C sits between 0°C = 32°F and 100°C = 212°F, and 140°F is indeed between them. On the pyrometer, the deciding fact is not the material but the range: above about 1200°C contact instruments fail, so non-contact is forced.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Convert to metres and kg/m3 before substituting. ρgh only returns pascals if h is in metres.
  • The diameter in A = πd2/4 must be in metres, because the area is needed in m2 to give a discharge in m3/s.
  • ρ of mercury is 13600, not 13.6. The 13.6 is specific gravity, and using it in ρgh gives an answer three orders off.
  • Remember the square root in √(2gh). Without it the velocity is 2gh instead of √(2gh).
  • Pitot gives velocity, venturi gives discharge. Do not apply the discharge formula to a pitot reading.
  • Torque is force times radius in METRES. A radius left in millimetres gives a torque a thousand times too large.
  • Manometer gives the pressure DIFFERENCE, not an absolute pressure. A U-tube measures Δp.
  • Above about 1200°C the answer is pyrometer, and the reason is non-contact, not accuracy.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Temperature, pressure, velocity, flow, force and torque are each measured by an instrument working on a definite physical principle which converts the quantity into an easier one to read, such as a length, a deflection or a voltage.”
  • Pairing line — “Temperature is measured by expansion in a thermometer, by thermoelectric emf in a thermocouple, and by radiation in a pyrometer; pressure by a liquid column in a manometer and by elastic deflection in a Bourdon tube.”
  • Flow line — “Flow is measured by the variable-head principle in the venturi and orifice meters, where the discharge follows Q = CdA√(2gh), and by the variable-area principle in the rotameter.”

Statement to write verbatim

Each of these quantities is measured by an instrument that converts it
into something easier to read:

  TEMPERATURE -> expansion (liquid-in-glass), emf (thermocouple),

                 (Bourdon tube, diaphragm)

  TORQUE      -> force at a known radius, T = F x r; or angle of twist

VENTURI vs ORIFICE
  Orifice: cheap, compact, LARGE permanent pressure loss.
  Venturi: costly, bulky, SMALL permanent pressure loss (recovers head).

Derivation in four steps

1. Pressure  -> a column of fluid balances the pressure:
                p = rho g h          [h in METRES, rho in kg/m3]
2. Velocity  -> the pitot head is the velocity head:
                v = sqrt(2 g h)
3. Flow      -> throttle the flow, read the head difference:
                Q = Cd A sqrt(2 g h)   [A in m2, d in METRES]
4. Torque    -> measure force at a known lever arm:
                T = F x r              [r in METRES]

Definitions to reproduce

  • Thermocouple — two dissimilar metals joined at a hot junction, generating an emf proportional to the temperature difference between it and the cold junction.
  • Pyrometer — a non-contact instrument measuring temperature from the radiation emitted by the hot body; the only type usable above about 1200°C.
  • Manometer — an instrument measuring a pressure difference by the height of a liquid column it supports; p = ρgh.
  • Bourdon tube — a curved, flattened tube of oval section that tends to straighten under internal pressure, the movement being transmitted to a needle.
  • Pitot tube — a probe measuring the difference between the total and static pressure in a flowing fluid, giving the velocity as √(2gh).
  • Venturi meter — a variable-head flow meter with a gradual contraction, throat and gradual expansion, recovering most of the pressure head.
  • Orifice meter — a variable-head flow meter consisting of a flat plate with a sharp-edged hole, cheap but with a large permanent pressure loss.
  • Rotameter — a variable-area meter in which a float rises in a tapered tube until the annular area balances the flow.
  • Coefficient of discharge — the ratio of actual discharge to the theoretical discharge, about 0.6 to 0.98.
  • Strain gauge — a wire or foil element bonded to a member, whose electrical resistance changes as the member strains.
  • Torque — the turning moment of a force about an axis, T = Fr, measured by a dynamometer or a torsion meter.

Explained in full — the optical pyrometer. The pyrometer consists of a telescope fitted with a red filter, through which the observer sights the hot body, and a calibrated incandescent filament lamp in the same field of view. The filament current is adjusted by a rheostat until the filament brightness exactly matches the brightness of the hot body, so the filament disappears against it. The filament current then indicates the temperature on a calibrated scale.

The essential property is that the instrument is non-contact. It measures the radiation leaving the body rather than its temperature directly, so it neither melts at the temperature being measured nor draws heat from the body and cools it. That is why it is the only practical method above about 1200°C, and why it is used for furnaces, molten metal and incandescent sources.

Exam close: "Hence the choice of instrument is decided by the range and by whether contact is permissible — and the pyrometer exists precisely because above about 1200 degrees Celsius no contact instrument survives and contact would itself disturb the body being measured."

Two principles. In the variable-head instruments — the venturi meter and the orifice meter — the flow is throttled and the resulting pressure difference is measured, the discharge following Q = CdA√(2gh). In the variable-area instrument — the rotameter — a float rises in a tapered tube until the annular area is just sufficient to pass the flow, and the discharge is read directly off a scale with no calculation.

PointOrifice meterVenturi meter
ConstructionA flat plate with a sharp-edged holeA gradual conical contraction, a cylindrical throat and a gradual conical expansion
CostLow — simple to makeHigh — the profile must be accurately machined
SpaceCompact; fits between existing flangesBulky; needs a long straight length of pipe
Permanent pressure lossLarge — the sudden contraction and expansion dissipate energy as eddiesSmall — the streamlined passage largely recovers the pressure
AccuracyLower, and affected by edge wearHigher and more stable, since the profile does not wear sharply

Exam close: "Hence the orifice meter is cheap to install and wasteful to run, while the venturi meter is costly to install and efficient to run, so the choice is governed by the relative weight of installation cost and pumping cost."

Model answer · 5 marks

A mercury manometer shows a difference of 250 mm. Find the pressure difference. A venturi of 50 mm throat with Cd = 0.62 carries a head of 0.5 m — find the discharge.

Two numerals, both reducible to a unit conversion followed by one substitution. Write the conversion explicitly, since that is where the marks are lost.

   PART 1 - MANOMETER                  PART 2 - VENTURI
   ==================                  ================

   h = 250 mm                          d = 50 mm
     = 0.250 m  <-- CONVERT              = 0.050 m  <-- CONVERT
   rho = 13600 kg/m3                   A = pi d^2 / 4
   g = 9.81 m/s2                         = pi (0.050)^2 / 4
                                         = 0.0019635 m2
   p = rho g h
     = 13600 x 9.81 x 0.250            sqrt(2gh) = sqrt(2x9.81x0.5)
     = 33354 Pa                                  = 3.132 m/s
     = 33.35 kPa
                                       Q = Cd A sqrt(2gh)
                                         = 0.62 x 0.0019635 x 3.132
   RULE OF THUMB:                        = 0.003813 m3/s
   1 mm Hg ~ 133 Pa                       = 3.81 litres/s
   250 mm -> ~33250 Pa  = 33.25 kPa
   (within 0.3% - confirms it)         SANITY: a 50 mm throat at
                                       0.5 m head flows a few L/s

Part one — manometer. Given: h = 250 mm, ρ = 13600 kg/m3, g = 9.81 m/s2. Find: Δp. Formula: Δp = ρgh. Solve:

h = 0.250 m

p = 13600 x 9.81 x 0.250
  = 33354 Pa = 33.35 kPa

Part two — venturi discharge. Given: d = 50 mm, Cd = 0.62, h = 0.5 m. Find: Q. Formula: Q = CdA√(2gh), A = πd2/4. Solve:

d = 0.050 m
A = pi x (0.050)^2 / 4 = 0.0019635 m2
sqrt(2gh) = sqrt(2 x 9.81 x 0.5) = 3.132 m/s
Q = 0.62 x 0.0019635 x 3.132 = 0.003813 m3/s = 3.81 L/s

Answer: pressure difference 33.35 kPa; discharge 0.00381 m3/s (3.81 L/s).

Check: part one substitutes back to 33354/(13600 × 9.81) = 0.250 m, the stated head, and independently 250 × 133 = 33250 Pa by the 1 mm Hg ≈ 133 Pa rule, within 0.3%. Part two substitutes back to 0.003813/(0.62 × 0.0019635) = 3.132 m/s, the assumed velocity head. Both answers also pass a magnitude test: a mercury manometer of a few hundred millimetres gives a few tens of kPa, and a 50 mm throat under a 0.5 m head gives a few litres per second. Had either conversion been missed the results would have been a thousand or a million times out, and those magnitudes are immediately absurd.

Exam close: "Hence both quantities reduce to a single substitution once the units are taken in metres, the manometer giving 33.35 kPa from the column head and the venturi 3.81 litres per second from the throat area and head."
