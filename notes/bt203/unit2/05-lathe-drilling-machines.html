Lesson · BT203 Unit 2 — Measurement & Production

Lathe and Drilling Machines — Parts, Operations, and the Two Numericals

RGPV asks these as a pair in 4 papers each, always in the same shape: name the main parts and give the operations. The marks come from a labelled sketch and a list of operations with what each is for. Only two calculations ever appear — spindle speed from cutting speed, and machining time — so once the parts are drawn, the topic is finished.

The one-sentence statement

The lathe is a machine tool that produces a cylindrical surface by rotating
the work against a single-point cutting tool; the drilling machine produces a
hole by rotating the tool against a stationary work. On the lathe the WORK
turns; on the drilling machine the TOOL turns -- and that inversion is the
distinction every question is testing.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Both machines do the same thing — bring a rotating edge against material — and they differ only in which of the two rotates. That single difference is why a lathe makes round outside surfaces and a drill makes round inside ones.”

Lathe — the main parts

Knurling is not cutting. Every other operation in that table removes metal. Knurling displaces it — the tool presses a pattern into the surface, so the diameter grows slightly rather than shrinking. Naming knurling as a forming operation rather than a cutting one is a reliable extra mark.

Drilling machine operations

OperationWhat it does
DrillingProduces a hole with a twist drill
ReamingSizes and finishes an existing hole to a close tolerance with a multi-edge reamer
BoringEnlarges a hole to an accurate size, correcting position and straightness

Counterbore against countersink

Both seat a fastener head, and they are the pair examiners contrast. A counterbore is a flat-bottomed cylindrical recess for a socket-head cap screw, which needs a flat seat. A countersink is a conical recess for a countersunk screw, whose head is itself conical. Square recess for a square-shouldered head, taper recess for a taper head — the geometry of the head dictates the geometry of the recess.

Numericals in great depth

Only two calculations appear on these machines, and both are on the lathe. Spindle speed comes from the cutting speed; machining time comes from the feed and the speed.

Five-line answer format — machine numerals 
Given:  diameter in mm, cutting speed in m/min, feed in mm/rev
Find:   spindle speed N, or machining time T, or the set-over S
Formula:N = 1000 V / (pi D) ;  T = L / (f N) ;  S = L(D1-D2)/(2l)
Solve:  keep the 1000 in place, cancel the units, state rpm or min
Check:  N must come out in the low hundreds for steel; if it is in
        the thousands the 1000 has been dropped

Solved A — spindle speed from cutting speed

A mild steel bar of 50 mm diameter is to be turned at a cutting speed of 30 m/min. Find the spindle speed.

Given: D = 50 mm, V = 30 m/min. Find: N. Formula: N = 1000 V / (π D). Solve:

N = 1000 x 30 / (pi x 50)
  = 30000 / 157.08
  = 190.99 rpm
  ~ 191 rpm

Answer: the spindle speed is approximately 191 rpm.

Check: substituting back, π × 50 × 191 / 1000 = 30.0 m/min, returning the stated cutting speed. Independent magnitude check: 191 rpm is a normal speed for a 50 mm bar in mild steel. Had the 1000 been dropped, the answer would have been 0.19 rpm, which no machine runs at — so the placement of the 1000 is the thing to verify, and the sanity band for steel is a few hundred rpm, not a few thousand.

Solved B — tailstock set-over for taper turning

A taper is to be turned on a workpiece 200 mm long, the tapered portion being 100 mm long with diameters 40 mm and 30 mm at its two ends. Find the tailstock set-over required, and the half-angle of the taper.

The set-over is applied over the whole length of the work, but the taper is cut over only part of it — which is why two different lengths appear in the formula.

Given: L = 200 mm (total work), l = 100 mm (tapered length), D1 = 40 mm, D2 = 30 mm. Find: set-over S and half-angle α. Formula: S = L (D1 − D2) / (2 l); tan α = (D1 − D2) / (2 l). Solve:

S = 200 x (40 - 30) / (2 x 100)
  = 200 x 10 / 200
  = 10.00 mm

tan(alpha) = (40 - 30) / (2 x 100)
           = 10 / 200 = 0.05
alpha      = tan^-1 (0.05)
           = 2.862 deg          <- HALF angle

Answer: the tailstock must be set over by 10.00 mm, and the half-angle of the taper is 2.86°, giving an included angle of 5.72°.

Check: substituting back, L × tan α = 200 × 0.05 = 10.00 mm, recovering the set-over exactly. Second check on the two lengths: if the tapered portion had been the full 200 mm, the set-over would have been 200 × 10 / 400 = 5.00 mm, only half as much — so using the total length in place of the tapered length doubles the answer, which is the standard error on this numeral. Note also that 2.86° is a small angle, as a 10 mm difference over a 100 mm taper should be.

Solved C — machining time, drilling

A 40 mm deep hole is drilled at a spindle speed of 500 rpm with a feed of 0.2 mm per revolution. Find the machining time.

Given: depth L = 40 mm, f = 0.2 mm/rev, N = 500 rpm. Find: T. Formula: T = L / (f N). Solve:

feed rate = f x N = 0.2 x 500 = 100 mm/min

T = 40 / 100
  = 0.40 min
  = 24 seconds

Answer: the machining time is 0.40 min, that is 24 seconds.

Check: substituting back, 100 mm/min × 0.4 min = 40 mm, recovering the hole depth. Independent second check on sense: at 0.2 mm per revolution and 500 revolutions per minute the tool advances 100 mm each minute, so a 40 mm hole is a little under half a minute — and 24 seconds is indeed a little under thirty. Note the commonest error here: dividing L by f alone gives 200 min, and dividing by N alone gives 0.08 min. Both leave an obvious mismatch with the physical sense of a drilling operation, which is what the check is for.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • The 1000 in N = 1000 V/(πD) converts mm to m. Drop it and the speed comes out a thousand times too small.
  • D is in millimetres, V in metres per minute. Mixing them is what the 1000 exists to prevent.
  • Set-over uses the TOTAL work length and the TAPERED length, not the same length twice. S = L(D1−D2)/(2l) with L the whole job and l the tapered part.
  • The taper angle is the half-angle. Doubling it gives the included angle — same trap as the sine bar in topic 04.
  • Machining time divides by the product f N, not by f alone or N alone. The feed rate in mm/min is f × N.
  • Keep the units consistent. Feed in mm/rev and speed in rev/min give a feed rate in mm/min; a depth in mm then gives a time in minutes.
  • Spindle speed for steel is a few hundred rpm, not a few thousand. Use that band to catch a dropped 1000.
  • Knurling is not a cutting operation. It displaces metal rather than removing it.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “A lathe is a machine tool which produces a cylindrical surface by rotating the workpiece against a single-point cutting tool, while a drilling machine produces a hole by rotating the drill against a stationary workpiece.”
  • Distinguishing line — “In the lathe the work rotates and the tool is stationary; in the drilling machine the tool rotates and the work is stationary, and that inversion is the essential difference between the two machines.”
  • Parts line — “The principal parts of a lathe are the bed, headstock, tailstock, carriage with its saddle, cross-slide, compound rest and tool post, and the apron with the feed rod and lead screw.”
  • Operation line — “The common lathe operations are turning, facing, chamfering, knurling, thread cutting, drilling, boring, taper turning and parting off; the common drilling machine operations are drilling, reaming, boring, counterboring, countersinking, spot facing and tapping.”
  • Formula line — “The spindle speed follows from the cutting speed as N = 1000 V/(πD), and the machining time from the feed and speed as T = L/(fN).”
  • Closing line — “Hence the two machines are complementary rather than alternative: the lathe generates a cylindrical surface outside or inside the work, and the drilling machine originates the hole that the lathe may then bore to size.”
Exam tips
  • Sketch both machines — two labelled diagrams, lathe and drill, cover most of the question
  • Put the rotation arrow on the work for the lathe and on the tool for the drill — that one annotational difference makes the contrast visible
  • Give operations as a table with a one-line purpose for each, not a bare list
  • Name the lead screw for thread cutting and the feed rod for turning — it is the detail that separates a good answer
  • State the two formulas even if the question asks for parts only; it costs a line and can earn a mark
  • Box the answer with its unit — rpm for speed, mm for set-over, min for time
  • Never leave blank — the part labels alone clear 4 of 7

Cheat-Sheet · BT203 Unit 2 Topic 05

Lathe and Drilling Machines — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

LATHE
  A machine tool producing a cylindrical surface by rotating the WORK
  against a single-point cutting tool.
  Parts: bed, headstock, tailstock, carriage (saddle, cross-slide,
  compound rest, tool post), apron, feed rod, lead screw.
  Operations: turning, facing, chamfering, knurling, thread cutting,
  drilling, boring, taper turning, grooving, parting off, forming.

DRILLING MACHINE
  A machine tool producing a hole by rotating the TOOL against a
  stationary workpiece.
  Parts: base, column, table, head, spindle and quill, feed mechanism.
  Operations: drilling, reaming, boring, counterboring, countersinking,
  spot facing, tapping, trepanning.

THE DIFFERENCE
  In the lathe the WORK rotates; in the drilling machine the TOOL
  rotates. Hence the lathe generates round surfaces and the drill
  originates holes.

Derivation in four steps

1. Cutting speed -> surface speed of work past the tool, V m/min
                    circumference = pi D, so
                    N = 1000 V / (pi D)        [1000 converts mm to m]
2. Feed rate    -> f mm/rev x N rev/min = f N mm/min
3. Machining time-> T = L / (f N)                  [L in mm, T in min]
4. Taper        -> S = L (D1 - D2) / (2 l)     [L total, l tapered]
                    tan(alpha) = (D1-D2)/(2l) -> alpha is the HALF angle

QuantityLatheDrilling machine
What rotatesThe workThe tool
What is stationaryThe toolThe work, clamped to the table
ProducesCylindrical and conical surfaces, threads, flat facesHoles, and the finishing of holes

Work holdingChuck, faceplate, between centresVice, clamps, angle plate on the table

Operations quick list. Lathe: turning, facing, chamfering, knurling, thread cutting, drilling, boring, taper turning, grooving, parting off, forming. Drilling machine: drilling, reaming, boring, counterboring, countersinking, spot facing, tapping, trepanning.

Definitions to reproduce

  • Lathe — a machine tool that removes metal by rotating the work against a single-point cutting tool, producing cylindrical, conical and threaded surfaces.
  • Bed — the cast-iron base with guideways on which the carriage and tailstock slide.
  • Headstock — the housing containing the spindle and gear train, which rotates the work.
  • Tailstock — the sliding unit at the far end that supports long work on a centre or holds a drill for axial holes.
  • Carriage — the assembly that holds and moves the tool, comprising the saddle, cross-slide, compound rest and tool post.
  • Compound rest — the part that swivels to any angle about a vertical axis, used for short taper turning and chamfering.
  • Lead screw — the threaded shaft that drives the carriage for thread cutting, as distinct from the feed rod used for ordinary turning.
  • Knurling — a forming operation in which a patterned tool presses a rough grip into the surface without removing metal.
  • Counterboring — producing a flat-bottomed cylindrical recess so that a bolt or screw head may sit flush.
  • Countersinking — producing a conical recess to suit a countersunk screw head.
  • Reaming — finishing an existing hole to an accurate size with a multi-edge reamer.
  • Spot facing — producing a flat seating surface around a hole on a rough casting.

Numeral shortcut

Three relations. Speed: N = 1000 V/(πD) — keep the 1000, and expect a few hundred rpm for steel. Time: T = L/(fN), where fN is the feed rate in mm/min. Taper: S = L(D1−D2)/(2l) with L the total length and l the tapered length, and remember the angle it gives is the half-angle. Finish by substituting back and checking the magnitude is physically sensible.

Spindle and quillThe spindle rotates the drill while the quill carries it and allows axial feed
Feed mechanismAdvances the drill into the work, by hand lever or by power feed

Operation 1 — reaming. An existing hole is finished to an accurate size and a smooth bore by a multi-edge reamer held in the spindle. Drilling leaves a hole slightly oversize and rough, and reaming corrects both; it removes very little material and cannot originate a hole.

Operation 2 — counterboring. A flat-bottomed cylindrical recess is cut at the mouth of an existing hole so that a bolt or socket-head screw head can sit below the surface. Its flat bottom matches the square shoulder of the head, which is why the recess is cylindrical rather than conical — the contrast with countersinking, which cuts a cone for a conical head.

Exam close: "Hence the drilling machine is the complement of the lathe: it originates and finishes holes by rotating the tool against a stationary workpiece, so its operations are all hole operations — drilling, reaming, boring, counterboring, countersinking, spot facing and tapping."

Model answer · 5 marks

A 50 mm bar is turned at 30 m/min and a 40 mm deep hole is drilled at 500 rpm with a 0.2 mm/rev feed. Find the spindle speed for turning and the machining time for drilling.

Two numerals from the two machines. Both are single substitutions, so the marks are in stating the formula, keeping the units and finishing with a magnitude check.

   SPINDLE SPEED                    MACHINING TIME
   from cutting speed               from feed and speed
   =================                =================
   N = 1000 V / (pi D)              feed rate = f x N
                                    T = L / (f x N)
   V in m/min  ->  x 1000
   D in mm     ->  the 1000            L in mm
                   reconciles them    f in mm/rev
                                      N in rev/min
   N = 1000 x 30 / (pi x 50)          feed rate = 0.2 x 500
     = 30000 / 157.08                   = 100 mm/min
     = 190.99 rpm                       T = 40 / 100
     ~ 191 rpm                            = 0.40 min
                                          = 24 seconds

   SANITY BAND: steel turns at a few hundred      SANITY: at 100 mm/min a
   rpm. 0.19 or 1910 means the 1000 slipped.      40 mm hole is under a minute

Part one — spindle speed. Given: D = 50 mm, V = 30 m/min. Find: N. Formula: N = 1000 V/(πD). Solve:

N = 1000 x 30 / (pi x 50)
  = 30000 / 157.08
  = 190.99 rpm ~ 191 rpm

Part two — machining time. Given: L = 40 mm, f = 0.2 mm/rev, N = 500 rpm. Find: T. Formula: T = L/(fN). Solve:

feed rate = f x N = 0.2 x 500 = 100 mm/min
T = 40 / 100 = 0.40 min = 24 seconds

Answer: spindle speed 191 rpm; machining time 0.40 min (24 s).

Check: both substitute back. For the speed, π × 50 × 191/1000 = 30.0 m/min, the stated cutting speed; and 191 rpm sits in the few-hundred band expected for steel, so the 1000 is in the right place. For the time, 100 mm/min × 0.40 min = 40 mm, recovering the depth; and a feed rate of 100 mm/min means a 40 mm hole takes a little under half a minute, which 0.40 min is. Had the working given 200 min or 0.08 min, the feed rate would have been divided by f or by N alone instead of by their product.

Exam close: "Hence the spindle speed follows from the cutting speed by N = 1000V/(πD), the 1000 reconciling millimetres with metres, and the machining time from the feed rate fN, so that the two numbers reported are 191 rpm and 0.40 minute."
