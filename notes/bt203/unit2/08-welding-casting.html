Lesson · BT203 Unit 2 — Measurement & Production

Welding, Casting and Patterns — Joining and Shaping

This is the production half of Unit 2, and it appears in 3 of 11 papers with the identical stem: “Define welding, classify welding processes, and explain arc welding” — December 2020 asked it as Q6b. Casting and patterns sit beside it. Both are classification questions with a sketch, so the marks come from the framework rather than from detail.

The one-sentence statement

Welding is the process of joining two similar or dissimilar metals by the
application of heat, pressure, or both, with or without a filler material,
so that the parts coalesce into one. It is classified into FUSION welding,
where the joint is melted (gas, arc, thermit, resistance), and PRESSURE or
solid-state welding, where the joint is not melted but is forced together
(forge, friction, ultrasonic). Casting is the process of pouring molten metal
into a mould cavity shaped by a PATTERN, and allowing it to solidify.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Welding takes two pieces and makes them one; casting takes one pour and makes a complex shape. Both start from the same question — how do you hold the metal while it solidifies?”

Arc welding is asked by name. In the December 2020 pattern the stem is “define, classify, and explain arc welding”. So the arc-welding explanation is worth preparing separately: an electric arc is struck between a coated consumable electrode and the workpiece; the heat of the arc melts both the electrode tip and the parent metal to form a molten pool; the electrode coating decomposes to give a shielding gas and a slag which exclude the atmosphere; the electrode is fed down as it is consumed, and the pool solidifies behind it into the weld. That is the full answer and it carries the process-specific marks.

Solved A — arc power and heat input

An arc weld is made at 25 V and 150 A, with the arc travelling at 200 mm/min. Find the arc power and the heat input per millimetre of weld.

Given: V = 25 V, I = 150 A, S = 200 mm/min. Find: P and H. Formula: P = VI; H = (VI × 60)/(1000 S). Solve:

P = 25 x 150 = 3750 W = 3.75 kW

H = (25 x 150 x 60) / (1000 x 200)
  = 225000 / 200000
  = 1.125 kJ/mm
  = 1125 J/mm

Answer: arc power 3.75 kW, heat input 1.125 kJ/mm.

Check: substituting back, 1.125 × 1000 × 200/60 = 3750 W, recovering the arc power from the heat input — which confirms the 60 and the 1000 are both in the right place. Independent magnitude check: a manual arc weld runs at a few kilowatts and delivers roughly 1 kJ per millimetre, so both figures sit in the normal band. Note also the sense of the relation: at a fixed power a faster travel speed spreads the same heat over more length, so the heat input falls — which is why 300 mm/min would give 0.75 kJ/mm.

Solved B — pattern size from shrinkage and machining

A casting is to finish at 200 mm in length. The shrinkage allowance is 1.5% and a machining allowance of 2 mm is required on the length. Find the pattern length.

Given: L = 200 mm, s = 1.5% = 0.015, m = 2 mm. Find: Lp. Formula: Lp = L(1 + s) + m. Solve:

Lp = 200 x (1 + 0.015) + 2
   = 200 x 1.015 + 2
   = 203 + 2
   = 205 mm

Answer: the pattern must be 205 mm long.

Check: reversing the allowance, 205 − 2 = 203 mm and 203/1.015 = 200.0 mm, recovering the finished size exactly — so the order of the two allowances is right. Second check on direction: a pattern must always be larger than the casting it produces, because the metal contracts on cooling and some surface is subsequently machined away; 205 is larger than 200, as it must be. Had the shrinkage been subtracted the pattern would have come out 197 mm, and the casting would finish undersize. Third check on order: the shrinkage percentage applies to the casting dimension, and the machining allowance is then added as a fixed length, which is why they are not simply added together first.

Solved C — draft allowance

A pattern is 50 mm deep and must be given a draft of 1.5° so that it withdraws cleanly. Find the extra width required on each side. State what would happen if no draft were provided.

Given: h = 50 mm, θ = 1.5°. Find: the extra width per side. Formula: extra width = h tan θ. Solve:

extra width = 50 x tan 1.5 deg
            = 50 x 0.02619
            = 1.31 mm per side

  so the pattern is 2.62 mm wider overall than the casting
  at the top of the 50 mm depth

Answer: the extra width is 1.31 mm per side (2.62 mm on the width overall).

Check: the sense is right — a draft adds material at the top and tapers down, so the pattern is widest at the face from which it is withdrawn. Note also that the allowance is an angle, not a length, so it cannot be added to the shrinkage as a fixed number: it depends on the depth. Doubling the depth to 100 mm would double the extra width to 2.62 mm per side, which is why a deep pattern needs a smaller draft angle for the same clearance. Without draft, the pattern would drag on the mould walls as it is withdrawn, and the sand would collapse into the cavity — giving a torn mould, a rough and oversize casting, and in the worst case a mould that cannot be withdrawn at all.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Cutting speed against travel speed. In H = (VI × 60)/(1000 S) the S is the arc travel speed in mm/min, not a cutting speed in m/min.
  • The 60 converts mm/min to mm/s. Arc power is in joules per second, so the speed must be per second for the division to give joules per millimetre.
  • Shrinkage is a percentage, machining is a length. They cannot simply be added; apply the percentage to the casting dimension first, then add the fixed allowance.
  • A pattern is always larger than the casting. If your answer is smaller, an allowance has been subtracted that should have been added.
  • Draft is an angle, not a length. The extra width is h tan θ, so it depends on the depth of the pattern.
  • Shrinkage values are small and metal-specific — about 1% for cast iron, up to about 2% for steel and some non-ferrous alloys. Do not invent a large one.
  • Higher travel speed means lower heat input. The relation is inverse, and a question that asks what happens when the speed is increased is testing exactly that.
  • Soldering and brazing are not fusion welding, because the base metal does not melt. They are filler-based joining processes distinguished by the filler's melting point.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Welding is the process of joining two similar or dissimilar metals by the application of heat, pressure, or both, with or without a filler material, so that they coalesce into a single piece.”
  • Classification line — “Welding processes are broadly classified into fusion welding, in which the joint faces are melted, comprising gas welding, arc welding, thermit welding and resistance welding; and pressure or solid-state welding, in which the joint is not melted but is forced together, comprising forge welding, friction welding and ultrasonic welding.”
  • Arc line — “In arc welding an electric arc is struck between a coated consumable electrode and the workpiece; the heat of the arc melts the electrode tip and the parent metal to form a molten pool, the coating decomposing to provide a shielding gas and a slag which exclude the atmosphere, and the electrode being fed down as it is consumed.”
  • Soldering line — “Soldering and brazing are not welding, because the base metal does not melt; they are distinguished from each other by the melting point of the filler, below and above about 450°C respectively.”
  • Casting line — “Casting is the process of pouring molten metal into a mould cavity formed by a pattern, and allowing it to solidify; the pattern is made larger than the casting to allow for shrinkage, machining, draft and distortion.”

Definitions to reproduce

  • Welding — the process of joining two similar or dissimilar metals by heat, pressure or both, with or without a filler, so that they coalesce into one piece.
  • Fusion welding — welding in which the joint faces are melted, with or without a filler; includes gas, arc, thermit and resistance welding.
  • Pressure or solid-state welding — welding in which the joint is not melted but is forced into intimate contact, sometimes with local heating.
  • Arc welding — fusion welding in which heat is obtained from an electric arc struck between a coated electrode and the workpiece.
  • Soldering — joining with a filler that melts below about 450°C, the base metal remaining solid.
  • Brazing — joining with a filler that melts above about 450°C, the base metal again remaining solid.
  • Casting — the process of pouring molten metal into a mould cavity and allowing it to solidify into the required shape.
  • Pattern — the replica of the casting, made oversize by the allowances, used to form the mould cavity.
  • Shrinkage allowance — the extra size given to the pattern to compensate for the contraction of the metal as it cools, about 1 to 2%.
  • Machining allowance — extra stock left on surfaces that will be cut after casting.
  • Draft allowance — a slight taper given to the pattern so that it may be withdrawn without damaging the mould.
  • Core — a shaped body placed in the mould to form an internal cavity in the casting.
  • Fettling — the removal of runners, risers, flash and sand from the casting after shakeout.

Arc welding. In arc welding, an electric arc is struck between a coated consumable electrode and the workpiece. The heat of the arc — at several thousand degrees — melts both the tip of the electrode and the parent metal beneath it, forming a molten pool. The coating on the electrode performs two functions: it decomposes in the heat to release a shielding gas that excludes the atmosphere from the pool, and it forms a slag which floats on the surface of the molten metal and protects it as it cools. The electrode is fed downwards as it is consumed, and the arc is moved along the joint, so that the molten pool solidifies behind it into the weld bead.

Its advantages are that it needs no gas cylinders, is portable, and works in the open air, which is why it dominates structural and site work. Its limitation is that the slag must be chipped away after each pass, and the process is slower and less clean than the gas-shielded variants.

Exam close: "Hence the classification of welding turns on whether the joint itself is melted, and arc welding belongs to the fusion class, the arc serving as the heat source and the electrode coating as the means of excluding the atmosphere from the molten pool."

   WHY THE PATTERN IS BIGGER THAN THE CASTING
   ==========================================
   pattern size = finished size + SHRINKAGE + MACHINING + DRAFT (+ shake)

    200 mm  casting                     Lp = 200 x 1.015 + 2
      x 1.015 shrinkage                     = 203 + 2
      = 203 mm                              = 205 mm  -> the PATTERN
      + 2 mm  machining
      = 205 mm                                          205 > 200  OK

Definition. Casting is the process of pouring molten metal into a mould cavity and allowing it to solidify into the required shape. The cavity is formed by a pattern, which is a replica of the casting made oversize to allow for contraction and subsequent machining.

StepWhat happens
1. Pattern makingA pattern is made slightly larger than the finished casting
2. MouldingSand is packed around the pattern, which is then withdrawn to leave a cavity
3. Core makingA core is placed in the mould where an internal cavity is required
4. Melting and pouringMetal is melted and poured into the mould cavity
5. SolidificationThe casting solidifies and contracts in the mould
6. Shakeout and fettlingThe mould is broken up and runners, risers and flash are removed

Pattern allowances. Shrinkage allowance compensates for the contraction of the metal as it cools, about 1 to 2%. Machining allowance leaves extra stock on surfaces to be cut later. Draft allowance is a slight taper so that the pattern may be withdrawn without damaging the mould. Shake allowance compensates for the pattern being rapped to free it before withdrawal, and distortion allowance for the warping of awkward shapes on cooling.

Numeral. Given: L = 200 mm, s = 1.5% = 0.015, m = 2 mm. Find: the pattern length. Formula: Lp = L(1 + s) + m. Solve:

Lp = 200 x (1 + 0.015) + 2
   = 203 + 2
   = 205 mm

Answer: the pattern length is 205 mm.

Check: reversing the allowances, 205 − 2 = 203 and 203/1.015 = 200.0 mm, recovering the finished size, so the order of the two allowances is correct. The direction check is the important one: a pattern is always larger than the casting, because the metal contracts and some surface is machined away afterwards — and 205 is larger than 200. Had the shrinkage been subtracted the pattern would have come out 197 mm and every casting from it would finish undersize.

Exam close: "Hence the pattern must be larger than the casting by the sum of its allowances, and of those the shrinkage is a percentage applied to the casting dimension while the machining allowance is a fixed length added after it, giving a pattern of 205 mm for a 200 mm casting."
