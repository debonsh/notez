Lesson · BT203 Unit 1 — Materials

Alloy Steels and Their Applications — What Each Element Is For

A carbon steel runs out of properties. You cannot make it stainless, you cannot keep it strong at 600°C, and you cannot stop it wearing out in a crusher. Adding a few percent of another metal fixes each of those — and the reason is always the same: the alloying element interferes with something carbon already does. Learn what each element attacks, and the applications stop being a list to memorise.

The one-sentence statement

An alloy steel is a carbon steel containing small additions of elements
such as Cr, Ni, Mo, V, W or Mn, added to impart properties that carbon
alone cannot give -- corrosion resistance, high-temperature strength,
hardenability, wear resistance or toughness.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Each alloying element is added for one reason — to interfere with a specific weakness of plain carbon steel.”

The 12% rule. Stainless steel is not stainless because it contains a lot of alloy — it is stainless because chromium exceeds about 12%, which is the minimum for a continuous, adherent, self-healing Cr2O3 film. Below 12% the film breaks up and the steel rusts. If a question asks why 18-8 is stainless, that percentage is the answer.

Solved A — an 18-8 stainless batch

A heat of 18-8 stainless steel weighs 5 kg and contains 18% chromium, 8% nickel, the balance being iron. Find the mass of each element.

Given: m = 5 kg, pCr = 18%, pNi = 8%. Find: mass of each. Formula: me = m × p / 100. Solve:

m_Cr = 5 x 18 / 100 = 0.90 kg
m_Ni = 5 x  8 / 100 = 0.40 kg
m_Fe = 5 x 74 / 100 = 3.70 kg

Answer: chromium 0.90 kg, nickel 0.40 kg, iron 3.70 kg. The chromium figure is the one that matters: 0.90 kg in the batch is 18% by mass, comfortably above the 12% threshold, so this grade does form the continuous oxide film.

Check: 0.90 + 0.40 + 3.70 = 5.00 kg, exactly the batch weight. Independent second check: 0.90 / 5 × 100 = 18%, returning the input value; and 18% + 8% = 26%, leaving 74% iron, so 5 × 0.74 = 3.70 kg agrees with the subtraction route. Two routes, same answer.

Solved B — chromium to add, where the denominator moves

How much chromium must be added to 100 kg of steel to give an alloy containing 2% chromium?

The percentage is taken over the final mass, which now includes the chromium being added.

Given: m = 100 kg, target pCr = 2%. Find: x, chromium to add. Formula: x / (m + x) = p / 100. Solve:

x / (100 + x) = 0.02
x = 0.02 (100 + x) = 2 + 0.02 x
x (1 - 0.02) = 2
x = 2 / 0.98 = 2.0408 kg

Answer: add 2.04 kg of chromium; the resulting alloy weighs 102.04 kg.

Check: substitute back — 2.0408 / 102.0408 × 100 = 2.000%, exactly the target. Independent check by ratio: in a 2% Cr alloy the chromium-to-iron ratio is 2 : 98, so 100 kg of iron needs 100 × 2 / 98 = 2.0408 kg. Note also that this alloy, at 2% chromium, is nowhere near the 12% needed for stainless — a useful reminder that an alloyed steel is not automatically a stainless steel.

Solved C — rail steel from the wagon angle

A wagon axle is made of steel containing 1.2% carbon. A heat of 1000 kg is cast. Find the mass of carbon and of iron in the heat, and state why a rail grade uses a higher manganese addition than a plain structural steel.

Given: m = 1000 kg, pC = 1.2%. Find: masses, and the reason for the manganese. Formula: me = m × p / 100. Solve:

m_C  = 1000 x 1.2 / 100 = 12 kg
m_Fe = 1000 x 98.8 / 100 = 988 kg

Answer: carbon 12 kg, iron 988 kg. Manganese is added because a rail takes repeated wheel impact, and manganese both increases hardenability and combines with sulphur to form harmless manganese sulphide; without it the rail would have low notch toughness and would shatter rather than deform. A structural beam carries static load and needs none of that.

Check: 12 + 988 = 1000 kg exactly. Second check: 12 / 1000 × 100 = 1.2%, the stated carbon. The scale is right too — 1.2% carbon is high-carbon territory (topic 02's 0.60–1.0% band, slightly above it), which is exactly what a wear surface needs.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • The add-to denominator. For “how much chromium to add” the percentage is over the final mass. 0.02 × 100 = 2 kg gives 1.96%, not 2%.
  • 12% is not 18%. 18-8 is the composition; 12% is the threshold above which the oxide film becomes continuous. Questions ask about the threshold.
  • Percent by mass, not by volume. A 5 kg heat is a mass balance; do not attempt a volume balance.
  • Watch for percentages that do not add to 100. If a question lists 18% Cr and 8% Ni, the balance is 74% Fe — do not assume a further unspecified element is present.
  • Distinguish addition from composition. “Contains 1.2% carbon” is a composition statement; “1.2% carbon must be added” is a process question with a different denominator.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Alloy steels are carbon steels to which small percentages of elements such as chromium, nickel, molybdenum, vanadium or tungsten are added to impart properties carbon alone cannot provide.”
  • Significance line — “Each element is added to defeat one specific weakness — chromium against corrosion, molybdenum against tempering, tungsten against red-hot softening — so alloy design is the choice of which failure to prevent.”
  • Method line — “A composition is always a mass balance, so the mass of one element is the batch mass multiplied by its percentage over 100, and the percentage of an added element is taken over the final, larger mass.”
  • Limitation line — “Alloying raises cost and often lowers weldability, so a stainless or tool grade is specified only where plain carbon steel would actually fail.”
  • Closing line — “Hence 18-8 stainless is corrosion resistant because its 18% chromium exceeds the 12% needed for a continuous oxide film, and that is the single number that identifies the grade.”
Exam tips
  • Element to effect table first — one row per element, before any prose.
  • Give the mechanism, not the adjective — “chromium forms a Cr2O3 film” scores; “chromium prevents rust” does not.
  • Underline the keyword — corrosion, hardenability, red-hot hardness, toughness.
  • State the application with its reason — “rail steel, because it must resist wheel impact”.
  • Attempt order — this is the second-easiest Unit 1 question after classification; take it early.
  • Never leave blank — five elements with one line each clears 4 of 7.

Alloy Steels and Their Applications — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

An alloy steel is a carbon steel containing small additions of Cr, Ni, Mo,
V, W or Mn to impart properties carbon alone cannot give -- corrosion
resistance, high-temperature strength, hardenability, wear resistance
or toughness. Each element is added to defeat one specific weakness.

Derivation in four steps

1. Name the weakness      -> rusts / softens at heat / wears / brittle / weak
2. Name the element        -> Cr / Mo + Si / V / Ni / Mn
3. State the mechanism     -> Cr2O3 film / resists tempering / hard carbides /
                              stabilises austenite / raises hardenability
4. Name the application    -> stainless / boiler tube / tool / cryogenic / rail

Definitions to reproduce

  • Alloy steel — a steel containing, besides carbon, one or more elements added specifically to enhance mechanical or chemical properties.
  • Stainless steel — a steel containing at least about 12% chromium, which forms a continuous self-healing oxide film that prevents rusting.
  • 18-8 stainless — the commonest austenitic grade, 18% chromium and 8% nickel; the nickel keeps the structure austenitic so the film can repair itself.
  • Hardenability — the ability of a steel to harden through its section when quenched, raised mainly by molybdenum and manganese.
  • Red-hot hardness — the ability of a tool material to retain its hardness at elevated cutting temperature; the property tungsten is added for.

Checklist before you call the topic done

  • [ ] Can give the effect of Cr, Ni, Mo, Mn, V and W without hesitating
  • [ ] Can state the 12% chromium threshold and why it matters
  • [ ] Can name four named alloy steels with composition and application
  • [ ] Can explain what “red-hot hardness” means and which element gives it
  • [ ] Can say what manganese does that carbon does not
  • [ ] Can solve an add-to-denominator composition numeral

Numeral shortcut

Two moves only. Composition: multiply batch mass by the percentage over 100. Addition: solve x / (m + x) = p / 100, then divide x back by (m + x) to confirm the percentage. Finish by adding all the parts to the batch mass.

Model answer · 5 marks

What is stainless steel? Why is it stainless, and what is 18-8?

Three short parts in order: definition with the threshold, then the film mechanism, then the named grade. The threshold and the self-healing property are the two marks most often lost.

        the steel     the bare metal

        |  the scratch - steel |

   Cr below 12%  ->  film breaks up  ->  RUSTS

Definition: stainless steel is a steel containing at least about 12% chromium. 18-8 is the commonest austenitic grade, containing 18% chromium and 8% nickel.

Why: chromium reacts with oxygen to form a chromium oxide, Cr2O3, film on the surface. Above about 12% the film is continuous, adherent and self-healing — wherever it is scratched, fresh chromium re-oxidises and repairs the film. Because the film separates the steel from oxygen and water, the steel underneath does not corrode.

Role of the nickel: nickel stabilises the austenitic structure, which keeps the film able to repair itself and also raises low-temperature toughness, so 18-8 is usable for cryogenic service.

Exam close: "Stainlessness therefore depends on the chromium exceeding the 12% threshold rather than on the total alloy content, which is why an 18-8 grade resists corrosion and a 2% chromium alloy does not."

Why are molybdenum and tungsten added to alloy steels? Where are they used?

Both attack heat, but in different ways. Separate the two mechanisms clearly, then follow each to its application.

Molybdenum improves hardenability, the ability of a steel to harden through its whole section when quenched, and it resists the softening that tempering would otherwise cause. It also forms carbides that resist heat. That combination is why it appears in gears, crankshafts, ball bearings and high-temperature components.

Tungsten gives red-hot hardness. Its carbides are so stable that the cutting edge keeps its hardness at temperatures where a plain high-carbon tool steel would soften and break down. That is why it appears in high-speed cutting tools, drills, taps and dies.

Exam close: "Molybdenum is added for strength retained through the section, tungsten for a cutting edge that survives its own heat, which is why the two are never interchangeable."
