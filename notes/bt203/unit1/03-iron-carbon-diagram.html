Lesson · BT203 Unit 1 — Materials

The Iron-Carbon Diagram — The Map That Explains Every Steel

Dec-2020 Q6a asked you to explain this diagram, and it is the highest-value drawing in the whole unit. One graph carries four critical temperatures, four microconstituents and the entire explanation of why steel is tough and cast iron is brittle. Learn to draw it from memory and every steel question in the paper becomes an answer you have already written.

The one-sentence statement

The iron-carbon diagram maps the stable phases of the Fe-Fe3C system against
temperature and carbon content. It is used to predict the microconstituents
present in a steel at any condition, and hence its hardness, strength,
ductility and weldability.

The exam version of that statement is shorter and still scores: “A phase diagram showing the stable phases of an alloy as a function of temperature and composition, used to predict the structure and therefore the properties of the alloy.”

The skeleton to draw

Draw the diagram in this order and it comes out right every time. Axes first, then the two horizontal critical lines, then the steep Fe3C line, then the four regions between them.

The three figures that carry the marks. 0.77% C at 727°C is the eutectoid, 4.3% C at 1147°C is the eutectic, and 2.0% C is the steel / cast iron boundary. If you remember only these three plus the shape, you can draw the diagram in ninety seconds.

  • Ferrite (α) — body-centred cubic iron, soft and ductile. Dissolves at most 0.022% carbon at room temperature.
  • Austenite (γ) — face-centred cubic iron, soft and ductile. Dissolves up to 2.0% carbon at the eutectic temperature, which is why 2.0% is the steel boundary.
  • Cementite (Fe3C) — an intermetallic compound, not a solid solution. Extremely hard and brittle. Carries 6.67% carbon.
  • Pearlite — not a phase but a microconstituent: the lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite that forms when austenite crosses the A1 line. It is what makes steel strong rather than soft.
The distinction that earns a mark. Ferrite, austenite and cementite are phases. Pearlite is a microconstituent — a physical mixture of two phases. An answer that calls pearlite a phase is usually marked wrong on that line, so say “microconstituent” or “mixture of ferrite and cementite”.

Reading the diagram: hypo and hyper

Everything in the diagram follows from three compositions you already know from topic 02 — 0.022%, 0.77% and 2.0%.

Below 0.022% C—100% ferrite
0.022 – 0.77% CHypoeutectoid steelProeutectoid ferrite + pearlite
Exactly 0.77% CEutectoid steel100% pearlite
0.77 – 2.0% CHypereutectoid steelProeutectoid cementite + pearlite
2.0 – 4.3% CHypoeutectic cast ironProeutectic cementite + ledeburite/pearlite

Read it as one sentence: below 0.77% the extra carbon comes out as ferrite; above 0.77% it comes out as cementite; either way it joins pearlite. Ferrite is soft, so hypoeutectoid steel is the ductile one; cementite is brittle, so hypereutectoid steel is the hard, less workable one. That single sentence is why the diagram predicts properties.

The lever rule

Every numerical question on this diagram is the same two lines of algebra, read off the two phase boundaries on the tie line.

                        TIE LINE AT TEMPERATURE T
       C-alpha  <------ C0 ------>  C-Fe3C
        (0.022%)                (6.67%)

   % of the RIGHT phase   = (C0 - C-left)  / (C-right - C-left)  x 100
   % of the LEFT  phase   = (C-right - C0) / (C-right - C-left)  x 100

   The two always sum to exactly 100%.

The mnemonic is opposite arms of the tie line. The distance from the composition to one end gives the fraction of the phase at the other end. Get that backwards and you will still get two numbers that sum to 100 — so always check the sum, and check which phase the bigger number belongs to.

Solved A — 0.4% steel at 700°C

A steel containing 0.4% carbon is cooled to 700°C. Calculate the percentage of ferrite and cementite present.

At 700°C the alloy sits in the α + Fe3C region, below A1. The tie line therefore runs from 0.022% C to 6.67% C.

Given: C0 = 0.4%. Find: % ferrite, % cementite. Formula: lever rule. Solve:

% ferrite  = (6.67 - 0.4) / (6.67 - 0.022) x 100
           = 6.27 / 6.648 x 100 = 94.31%

% Fe3C     = (0.4 - 0.022) / (6.67 - 0.022) x 100
           = 0.378 / 6.648 x 100 = 5.69%

Answer: 94.31% ferrite and 5.69% cementite. Almost all ferrite, so this steel is soft and ductile.

Check: 94.31 + 5.69 = 100.00% exactly. Independent sanity check — the alloy is at 0.4% carbon, which is well to the iron end of the tie line (0.022 to 6.67), so the iron-rich phase must dominate by a wide margin. 94% is the right order of magnitude; a result like 40% would have signalled a reversed lever rule.

Solved B — 0.8% steel just below 727°C

A steel containing 0.8% carbon is cooled to just below 727°C. Calculate the percentage of proeutectoid cementite and pearlite.

0.8% is above 0.77%, so this steel is hypereutectoid and the tie line runs from the eutectoid point to the cementite boundary — a different pair of endpoints from Solved A. That single change is the whole trap.

Given: C0 = 0.8%. Find: % proeutectoid Fe3C, % pearlite. Formula: lever rule on the 0.77 / 6.67 tie line. Solve:

% proeutectoid Fe3C = (0.8 - 0.77) / (6.67 - 0.77) x 100
                     = 0.03 / 5.90 x 100 = 0.51%

% pearlite           = (6.67 - 0.8) / (6.67 - 0.77) x 100
                     = 5.87 / 5.90 x 100 = 99.49%

Answer: 0.51% proeutectoid cementite and 99.49% pearlite. Almost pure pearlite — hard, strong and less ductile than the 0.4% steel.

Check: 0.51 + 99.49 = 100.00%. Second check, and it is the useful one: the excess carbon above the eutectoid point is only 0.8 − 0.77 = 0.03% out of a 5.90% span, i.e. about 0.5%. A steel just past the eutectoid point should be almost entirely pearlite; anything near 50% would mean the endpoints were misread.

Solved C — 3.0% cast iron at room temperature

A cast iron containing 3.0% carbon is at room temperature. Calculate the percentage of ferrite and cementite, and comment on its behaviour in a tensile test.

Below 727°C every iron-carbon alloy relaxes into the same α + Fe3C field, so the tie line is again 0.022% to 6.67% — even though 3.0% carbon is a cast iron, not a steel.

Given: C0 = 3.0%. Find: % ferrite, % cementite. Formula: lever rule. Solve:

% ferrite  = (6.67 - 3.0) / (6.67 - 0.022) x 100
           = 3.67 / 6.648 x 100 = 55.20%

% Fe3C     = (3.0 - 0.022) / (6.67 - 0.022) x 100
           = 2.978 / 6.648 x 100 = 44.80%

Answer: 55.20% ferrite and 44.80% cementite. Nearly half the structure is cementite, which is hard and brittle, so the casting shows almost no ductility and will fail suddenly in a tensile test with negligible necking.

Check: 55.20 + 44.80 = 100.00%. Independent check by comparison: the same lever line gave 5.69% cementite for 0.4% carbon in Solved A. Raising carbon from 0.4% to 3.0% moved the composition much nearer the cementite end of an unchanged tie line, so the cementite fraction must rise sharply — 5.69% to 44.80% is exactly that. Order of magnitude and direction both agree.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Wrong tie-line endpoints. Below A1 use 0.022 / 6.67. Between the eutectoid and cementite boundaries use 0.77 / 6.67. Reading 0.022 / 0.77 in a pearlite region is the single most common error on this topic.
  • Reversed arms. The percentage of a phase comes from the distance to the opposite boundary. Both answers will still sum to 100%, so the sum alone will not catch it — check which phase the larger number belongs to.
  • Rounding before adding. Keep full precision until the final line. 94.31 + 5.69 = 100.00; 94.3 + 5.7 works too, but 94 + 5 = 99 shows the rounding was too coarse.
  • Treating pearlite as a phase. It is a microconstituent, a mixture of ferrite and cementite. Only three phases exist on this diagram.
  • Mixing up 0.77 and 2.0. 0.77% is the eutectoid composition; 2.0% is the maximum carbon soluble in austenite, and therefore the steel / cast iron boundary. They are two different facts and both are examinable.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “The iron-carbon diagram is a phase diagram showing the stable phases of the Fe–Fe3C system as a function of temperature and carbon content.”
  • Significance line — “It is the single most used diagram in metallurgy because the microconstituents it predicts — ferrite, cementite, pearlite — directly fix the hardness, strength, ductility and weldability of the steel.”
  • Method line — “Any composition at a given temperature is obtained by the lever rule: draw a tie line across the two phase boundaries at that temperature and divide each arm against the full span.”
  • Limitation line — “The diagram describes equilibrium only; real cooling rates are fast enough to shift the transformation temperatures and leave non-equilibrium structures such as martensite and bainite, which this diagram cannot show.”
  • Closing line — “Hence the composition window 0.77% C at 727°C and 4.3% C at 1147°C divides the system, and the phase fractions follow from the lever rule on the tie line.”
Exam tips
  • Draw before you write — a labelled diagram is worth more than three paragraphs of prose and takes half the time.
  • Label every line — A1, A3, Acm, eutectoid 0.77 / 727, eutectic 4.3 / 1147. An unlabelled curve scores nothing.
  • Mark the four phases — α, γ, Fe3C, L, and pearlite as a microconstituent.
  • Underline the three magic numbers — 0.022, 0.77, 2.0, plus 4.3 and 6.67.
  • Attempt order — draw the diagram question first; it is high-value and self-contained.
  • Never leave blank — an axes-and-eutectoid-marked sketch with two written criticals clears 3 of 7 before you have written a sentence of prose.

  1. Axes: temperature in degrees Celsius against carbon content in percent by mass. The Fe–Fe3C system is described up to 6.67% carbon, which is the carbon content of cementite.
  2. Critical lines: A3 is the upper boundary of the austenite-to-ferrite transformation; Acm is the solubility limit of cementite in austenite; A1 lies horizontally at 727°C and is the eutectoid line.
  3. Phases: alpha ferrite, body-centred cubic and soft, holding at most 0.022% carbon; gamma austenite, face-centred cubic and ductile, holding up to 2.0% carbon; Fe3C cementite, extremely hard and brittle; and liquid L.

  5. Invariant points: the eutectoid at 0.77% C and 727°C, where austenite becomes pearlite; the eutectic at 4.3% C and 1147°C, where liquid becomes ledeburite.
  6. Amounts: the fraction of each phase in a two-phase field is read by the lever rule — a tie line across the two boundaries, with each arm divided against the full span.

Exam close: "The diagram is worth memorising because the microconstituents it predicts — ferrite, cementite, pearlite — directly fix the hardness, strength, ductility and weldability of any steel, which is why it is the most used diagram in metallurgy."

Model answer · 5 marks

What is pearlite? How is it formed, and why does it strengthen steel?

Define, give the formation route with both numbers, then explain the mechanism. The mechanism is the part students skip and it carries two marks.

      COOLING THROUGH A1 at 727 deg C
                    |
                    v
        +-----------------------+
        |  AUSTENITE (gamma)     |
        |  0.77% C, FCC, soft   |
        +-----------------------+
                    |
                    |  crosses A1 at 727 degC
                    v
        +-----------------------+
        |      PEARLITE         |
        |  lamellar mixture of  |
        |  ferrite + cementite  |
        +-----------------------+
                    |
       +------------+-------------+
       |            |             |
       v            v             v
  +---------+  +-----------+  +---------+
  | FERRITE |  | CEMENTITE |  | FERRITE |
  |  hard   |  |  soft,    |  |  hard   |
  |  matrix |  |  carries  |  |  matrix |
  |         |  |  the load |  |         |
  +---------+  +-----------+  +---------+
       \            |            /
        \           |           /
         v          v          v
       +-----------------------+
       |  LAMELLAE alternate -  |
       |  dislocations must    |
       |  cut BOTH phases, so  |
       |  plastic flow is      |
       |  resisted             |
       +-----------------------+

Formation: when a steel of eutectoid composition (0.77% carbon) cools through the A1 line at 727°C, austenite decomposes into the eutectoid mixture of ferrite and cementite, which appears under the microscope as alternating lamellae. That is pearlite.

Why it strengthens: pearlite combines a soft, ductile ferrite matrix with hard, brittle cementite lamellae spaced so closely that a dislocation moving through the structure must cut or bypass both phases. The lamellae therefore block slip, so far more stress is needed to produce plastic deformation than in pure ferrite.

Exam close: "Pearlite is therefore a microconstituent and not a phase, and it is the reason a plain carbon steel can be strong while remaining ductile."

        | at the edge, less weld-|
        | able, used for tools   |
        +-----------------------+
AspectHypoeutectoid steelHypereutectoid steel
Carbon range0.022 - 0.77%0.77 - 2.0%
Proeutectoid phaseFerrite, soft and ductileCementite, hard and brittle
Structure below A1Proeutectoid ferrite + pearliteProeutectoid cementite + pearlite
PropertiesMore ductile, more weldable, less hardHarder, wear resistant, less ductile, harder to weld
Typical useGears, shafts, structural partsTool steel, springs, cutting edges

The consequence follows directly from the phase. Proeutectoid ferrite is soft, so the hypo side stays ductile and readily weldable. Proeutectoid cementite is hard and brittle, so the hyper side gains hardness and wear resistance at the cost of ductility and weldability, which is exactly why high-carbon tool steels are annealed before machining.

Exam close: "Both are pearlite-based steels; the proeutectoid phase decides which side of the ductility trade-off the alloy sits on, and 0.77% carbon is the line."
