Lesson · BT203 Unit 1 — Materials

Hardness and Impact Testing (BHN) — Two Tests That Answer Different Questions

Dec-2020 Q1b asked “What is hardness? How is it measured?” and it is one of the most examinable questions in Unit 1, because it has a definition, three named tests, one formula and a trap that catches everyone. The trap is that hardness and toughness are not the same thing, and impact testing is the test that measures the second one.

The one-sentence statement

Hardness is the resistance of a material to indentation, scratching,
abrasion or cutting. It is measured by forcing a standard indenter
into the surface under a known load and reading the depth of penetration,
giving a hardness number such as BHN, Rockwell or VHN.

And the second sentence, which is what the Q1b half of the question wants: “Hardness must not be confused with toughness, which is the ability to absorb impact energy and is measured by the Izod or Charpy test.”

A steel ball — or a tungsten carbide ball for hard materials — of known diameter D is pressed into the surface with a known load P for a standard time, usually 10 to 15 seconds. A microscope measures the diameter d of the indentation. The hardness number is:

  BHN = 2P
        ---------
        pi . D . (D - d)

  P = applied load       [kgf]
  D = ball diameter      [mm]
  d = indentation dia.   [mm]

  Standard conditions:  P = 3000 kgf, D = 10 mm  ->  BHN up to ~600
                       P = 500 kgf,  D = 10 mm  ->  BHN up to ~200

How to choose in one line. Coarse, rough surface and moderate hardness → Brinell. Thin sheet or a hardened case needing a shallow indentation → Rockwell. Very hard material, a thin layer, or a research measurement → Vickers. Choosing wrongly costs the application mark.

Impact testing: Izod and Charpy

Hardness measures surface resistance to slow penetration. It tells you nothing about how a material behaves under a sudden load — and that is exactly what fails a railway coupler or a crankshaft. Impact testing fills the gap.

The ductile-to-brittle transition temperature is read by plotting impact energy against temperature: above the transition the material is ductile and absorbs high energy; below it it is brittle and absorbs very little. Rail steels and pressure vessels are tested for exactly this temperature, because a structure that is ductile in summer must not be brittle in winter.

Hardness versus toughness, one last time

This is the trap the topic is built around. A highly hardened steel is hard and usually not tough. Increasing hardness by quenching and tempering lowers toughness, so a very hard tool is deliberately made slightly less hard when it must also survive shock. If a question asks “why is a steel quenched from 800°C hard but brittle?” the answer is exactly this trade.

Solved A — the Brinell hardness number

A steel specimen is tested with a 10 mm ball under a load of 6000 kgf, producing an indentation of 0.6 mm diameter. Calculate the Brinell hardness number.

Given: P = 6000 kgf, D = 10 mm, d = 0.6 mm. Find: BHN. Formula: BHN = 2P / (π D (D − d)). Solve:

D - d   = 10 - 0.6 = 9.4 mm
pi . D . (D - d) = 3.1416 x 10 x 9.4 = 295.31

BHN = 2 x 6000 / 295.31
    = 12000 / 295.31
    = 40.64

Answer: BHN = 40.6 (about 41).

Check: the answer must be dimensionless, since P, D and d all share a compatible set of units — and it is, which confirms no unit error. Physical check: with d/D = 0.06 the indentation is very shallow, and a shallow indentation under a heavy load means a soft material. BHN 40 is in the range of mild steel or soft brass, well below the 200 to 300 of a hardened steel, so the number and the geometry tell the same story. Note the load here is twice the standard 3000 kgf, so this specimen is outside the normal Brinell range and the reading should be quoted with that caveat.

Solved B — impact energy density

An Izod test on a notched steel specimen absorbs 75 J. The cross-section at the notch is 5 cm2. Calculate the energy absorbed per unit area and comment on the material.

Given: E = 75 J, A = 5 cm2. Find: energy per unit area. Formula: e = E / A. Solve:

e = 75 / 5 = 15 J/cm^2

Answer: 15 J per cm2. For a steel, a value in this range indicates a tough material able to withstand shock, which is what is wanted for a coupling, a crankshaft or a wagon underframe.

Check: the units are joules per cm2, which is a strength of impact and not a total energy, so the answer must be smaller than 75 — and 15 is. Second check by reciprocation: 75 J over 5 cm2 means each square centimetre of notch absorbed 15 J; if the area had been 15 cm2 the answer would be 5 J/cm2, so the figure scales inversely with area as it must.

Solved C — solving backwards for the indentation

A material is tested with a 10 mm ball under 4000 kgf and gives a hardness of 220 BHN. Find the diameter of the indentation.

This is the Brinell formula run in reverse, and it is the version examiners like because rearranging the formula is the skill being tested.

Given: P = 4000 kgf, D = 10 mm, BHN = 220. Find: d. Formula: from BHN = 2P / (π D (D − d)), we get D − d = 2P / (π D × BHN), so d = D − 2P / (π D × BHN). Solve:

2P / (pi . D . BHN) = 8000 / (3.1416 x 10 x 220)
                    = 8000 / 6911.5
                    = 1.1575 mm

d = D - 1.1575 = 10 - 1.1575 = 8.8425 mm

Answer: the indentation diameter is 8.84 mm.

Check: substitute back into the Brinell formula: 2 × 4000 / (π × 10 × (10 − 8.8425)) = 8000 / (π × 10 × 1.1575) = 8000 / 36.366 = 219.99, which is the required 220 to within rounding. Independent physical check: d/D = 8.8425 / 10 = 0.884, a broad indentation, and a broad indentation under load is exactly what a hard material produces — the opposite of Solved A's 0.06 ratio. The two problems bracket the normal range: ratio 0.06 gives BHN 41, ratio 0.88 gives BHN 220.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • D minus d, not D plus d. The formula is 2P / (π D (D − d)). Using D + d gives a hardness roughly a fifth of the correct value.
  • Use the standard load. BHN is defined for 3000 kgf with a 10 mm ball; the 500 kgf variant is used only below about 200 BHN. A 6000 kgf reading like Solved A's is outside the normal range and should be flagged.
  • kgf, not kN. The classical Brinell formula takes the load in kilograms-force. Substituting newtons gives a number a thousand times too large.
  • Indenter choice. Steel ball for soft and medium materials, tungsten carbide for hardened steel; the number is not comparable across different ball materials.
  • Do not confuse the two tests. If the question mentions a pendulum and a notch, it is impact energy, not hardness; there is no indenter involved at all.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Hardness is the resistance of a material to indentation, scratching, abrasion or cutting, and it is measured by forcing a standard indenter into the surface under a known load and measuring the depth of penetration.”
  • Significance line — “Hardness is a surface property whereas toughness is a bulk property, so a surface-hardened part can be very hard and still have a soft, shock-absorbing core underneath.”
  • Method line — “The Brinell number is BHN = 2P / (π D (D − d)), evaluated for the standard 10 mm ball under 3000 kgf, and the impact energy is read directly from the machine in joules.”
  • Limitation line — “Because the tests rank materials only against each other, a hardness number cannot be compared between different indenters or across different loads.”
  • Closing line — “Hence a hardened steel is hard but not necessarily tough, and the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature found by the Charpy test is what decides whether it is safe to use at low temperature.”
Exam tips
  • Definition, then formula, then the three tests — in that order; it maps onto the marks.
  • Name the indenter for each test — ball, diamond cone, diamond pyramid; this is the mark most often lost.
  • State the load for each test — 3000 kgf Brinell, 150 kgf Rockwell C, 1 to 30 kgf Vickers.
  • Underline the keywords — hardness, toughness, Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers, Izod, Charpy.
  • Close with the hardness/toughness distinction — it is the part examiners ask for by name.
  • Never leave blank — a definition plus three test names clears 3 of 7.

Cheat-Sheet · BT203 Unit 1 Topic 08

Hardness and Impact Testing — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

Hardness is the resistance of a material to indentation, scratching,
abrasion or cutting. It is measured by forcing a standard indenter into
the surface under a known load and reading the depth of penetration.

Hardness is NOT toughness: hardness is a surface property, while
toughness is the ability to absorb impact energy, measured by the
Izod or Charpy test.

Derivation in four steps

1. Press the indenter  -> ball of diameter D under load P, for 10-15 s
2. Measure the dent    -> indentation diameter d, by microscope
3. Substitute          -> BHN = 2P / (pi . D . (D - d))
4. Choose the test     -> Brinell coarse | Rockwell thin/hardened | Vickers very hard

Definitions to reproduce

  • Hardness — resistance to indentation, scratching, abrasion or cutting; a surface property.
  • Brinell hardness number — 2P / (π D (D − d)), with P in kgf and D and d in mm.
  • Toughness — the ability to absorb energy up to fracture; a bulk property measured by the Charpy or Izod test.
  • Impact energy — the energy in joules absorbed in breaking a notched specimen in a single blow.
  • Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature — the temperature above which a steel absorbs high impact energy and below which it fractures in a brittle manner.

Checklist before you call the topic done

  • [ ] Can write the Brinell formula and define P, D and d with units
  • [ ] Can state the standard load as 3000 kgf with a 10 mm ball
  • [ ] Can name indenter, load and application for Brinell, Rockwell and Vickers
  • [ ] Can distinguish an Izod specimen from a Charpy specimen by orientation
  • [ ] Can state hardness versus toughness in one sentence with an example
  • [ ] Can rearrange the Brinell formula to solve for d

Numeral shortcut

The whole topic is one formula. BHN = 2P / (π D (D − d)). Compute D − d first, then the denominator, then divide. Use kgf, not newtons. To go the other way, D − d = 2P / (π D × BHN), then subtract from D. Always substitute back, and use a shallow indentation for a soft material and a broad one for a hard one as your physical check.

Hardness is not toughness. Hardness is a surface property, measured by indentation, whereas toughness is the ability to absorb energy and resist fracture, measured by the Izod or Charpy impact test. The two are in fact in tension with each other: a steel hardened by quenching is harder but also more brittle, so a tool that must also survive shock is deliberately left slightly softer. A file is extremely hard with almost no toughness; a rubber tyre is barely hard at all and very tough.

Ductile-to-brittle transition: if impact energy is plotted against temperature, the curve is high and nearly flat at high temperature — the material is ductile and absorbs a large amount of energy — and falls sharply to a low value below a critical temperature, where the material is brittle and absorbs very little. That critical temperature is the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature. Rail steels and pressure vessels are tested for it, because a structure that is ductile in summer must not become brittle in winter.

Exam close: "The transition temperature therefore fixes the lowest safe service temperature for a given steel, which is why it is specified for any component that sees impact loading in cold conditions."
