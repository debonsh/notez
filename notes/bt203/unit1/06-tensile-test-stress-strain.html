Tensile Test and Stress-Strain Diagrams — The Six Stages That Explain Every Number

Almost every genuine calculation in Unit 1 lives here. A tensile test is the one experiment that returns strength, ductility, stiffness, resilience and toughness at once, because it produces a single curve from which all five are read. Learn the six stages of the curve and the formulas fall out of it, instead of memorising formulas with no picture attached.

The one-sentence statement

The tensile test pulls a standard specimen axially until it fractures,
recording load against extension. The resulting stress-strain curve gives
the stiffness (E), the yield and ultimate strength, the ductility and the
toughness of the material in one graph.

The test

The specimen is a standard circular bar of diameter d0 with two marked points defining the original gauge length L0. Two dial gauges read the extension and the load cell reads the force; both are plotted against extension to give the load-extension curve, which is then converted to the stress-strain curve using the original area and gauge length.

From load-extension to stress-strain

stress   sigma = P / A0      A0 = (pi / 4) x d0^2   (ORIGINAL area)
strain   e     = delta L / L0                       (ORIGINAL gauge length)

stress-strain curve = load-extension curve with the axes rescaled

The word original appears twice and it is the whole convention. Engineering stress and engineering strain are both computed on the original dimensions, which is what makes them easy to calculate and what makes the curve well defined up to the ultimate point.

The six stages of a ductile curve

#StageWhat is happeningRead off it
1Proportional limitStress is directly proportional to strain; the graph is a straight lineThe slope of that line is Young's modulus E
2Elastic limitBeyond this the material will not return to its original lengthThe resilience, as the area under the curve up to this point
3Yield pointPlastic deformation begins at practically constant stressYield stress — the working stress a designer never exceeds
4Strain hardeningStress rises again as the material work-hardensUltimate tensile stress, the peak
5NeckingBeyond the peak the cross-section shrinks locallyTrue stress keeps rising even though engineering stress falls
6FractureThe specimen breaks at the neckToughness, the total area under the curve
Why the curve falls after the peak. The fall is an artefact, not a real weakening. Once necking starts the load drops because the area reduces faster than the stress increases. Measured on the instantaneous necked area — true stress — the curve keeps rising to fracture. If a question asks why engineering stress falls after UTS, that is the answer, and it is a free mark.

   no yield point, breaks almost

   under the curve = brittle
  • Brittle material: very steep elastic region, no yield point, no necking, low strain at fracture, and a very small area under the curve. It breaks suddenly, like glass or grey cast iron.
  • Ductile material: clear yield point, strain hardening, necking, large area under the curve, and a warning before failure.
  • Consequence for design: a ductile material warns before it fails; a brittle one gives no warning, which is why structures are made ductile and why impact testing exists.

Five-line answer format — every tensile-test numeral 
Given:  d0, d1, L0, L1 and the maximum load
Find:   stress, strain, % elongation or % reduction of area
Formula:A0 = pi/4 d0^2 ; sigma = P/A0 ; %EL or %RA as above
Solve:  find A0 (and A1) first, then the required quantity
Check:  %RA and %EL both measure ductility and should be of similar size

Solved A — stress and ductility of one specimen

A circular steel specimen of 12 mm diameter and 50 mm gauge length carries a maximum load of 30 kN. At fracture its gauge length is 62.5 mm and its diameter at the neck is 9 mm. Calculate the maximum engineering stress, the percentage elongation and the percentage reduction of area.

Given: d0 = 12 mm, L0 = 50 mm, P = 30 kN = 30,000 N, L1 = 62.5 mm, d1 = 9 mm. Find: σmax, %EL, %RA. Formula: as in the table. Solve:

A0 = (pi / 4) x 12^2 = 113.097 mm^2

sigma_max = 30000 / 113.097 = 265.26 MPa

% EL = (62.5 - 50) / 50 x 100 = 12.5 / 50 x 100 = 25.00%

% RA = (113.097 - 63.617) / 113.097 x 100
     = 49.480 / 113.097 x 100 = 43.75%
       where A1 = (pi / 4) x 9^2 = 63.617 mm^2

Answer: maximum engineering stress 265.26 MPa, percentage elongation 25.00%, percentage reduction of area 43.75%. Both ductility figures are high, so this is a ductile steel, and the reduction of area being the larger of the two is exactly what a necking ductile material should show.

Check: %RA can be computed from the diameters alone, because both areas carry the same π/4 factor: (144 − 81) / 144 × 100 = 63/144 × 100 = 43.75%, which agrees with the area route. Three cross-checks pass. The units cancel to N/mm2 = MPa. And the physical story is consistent: a material that stretches 25% must also show a measurable neck, and 43.75% reduction of area is what that neck looks like.

Solved B — why engineering stress falls after the peak

Continuing the same specimen: what is the true stress at fracture, and why is it so much higher than the engineering stress?

Given: P = 30,000 N, d1 = 9 mm, A0 = 113.097 mm2, σeng,max = 265.26 MPa. Find: σtrue. Formula: σtrue = P / A1. Solve:

sigma_true = 30000 / 63.617 = 471.57 MPa

ratio = A0 / A1 = 113.097 / 63.617 = 1.7778
  so sigma_true = 265.26 x 1.7778 = 471.56 MPa   (agrees)

Answer: the true stress at fracture is 471.57 MPa, which is 1.78 times the engineering stress.

Check: the second route gives 265.26 × 1.7778 = 471.56 MPa against 471.57 from the direct division — the 0.01 difference is rounding in A1, not arithmetic error. The direction is the important part: because A1 is smaller than A0, true stress must be higher. This is precisely why the engineering curve falls after the ultimate point while the true curve keeps rising.

Solved C — identifying a brittle material from one number

A specimen fractures with only 0.5% elongation and 3% reduction of area. Classify it and state what you would expect the stress-strain curve to look like.

Given: %EL = 0.5%, %RA = 3%. Find: classification and curve shape. Solve: both ductility measures are far below the 20% regarded as ductile for a ferrous metal, and both are small.

Answer: the material is brittle. Its curve will show a very steep linear elastic region, no yield point, no necking, and will break almost immediately after the elastic limit at a strain of only about 0.005.

Check: the two independent ductility measures agree with each other in being tiny — %RA = 3% against %EL = 0.5% is the expected relationship, since reduction of area concentrates the deformation into the neck. For comparison, Solved A gave 25% and 43.75% for a ductile steel, both roughly two orders of magnitude larger. The contrast is the check: no arithmetic was needed, and the classification is certain.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Engineering uses the original area. Stress is P / A0, never P / A1, unless the question explicitly says true stress.
  • The diameter may change. At fracture d1 is smaller than d0; using d0 for both %RA and the area route gives a wrong answer.
  • %RA from diameters only. (d02 − d12) / d02 × 100, since the π/4 cancels. Use this as your own check.
  • Units. A0 in mm2 with P in newtons gives stress directly in N/mm2 = MPa. If you get 265.26 with a decimal point in five digits, you have not converted; round to 265 MPa.
  • Joining the halves before measuring. Percentage elongation is measured between the two fractured halves joined end to end, not between the grips.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “The tensile test pulls a standard specimen axially to fracture while recording load against extension, and the rescaled curve gives stiffness, strength, ductility and toughness in one graph.”
  • Significance line — “The slope of the linear portion is Young's modulus, the elastic area is resilience and the whole area is toughness, so a single curve supplies every mechanical property of the material.”
  • Method line — “Engineering stress and strain are both computed on the original dimensions, A0 and L0, which is why the curve is well defined up to the ultimate point and why it appears to fall afterwards.”
  • Limitation line — “Because engineering stress uses a fixed area, it understates the true stress beyond necking and cannot be used for materials that fail without necking.”
  • Closing line — “Hence a ductile material gives a warning through necking before failure, while a brittle one breaks with almost no warning — so toughness is read from the area under the curve and is the reason impact testing exists at all.”
Exam tips
  • Draw the curve first — a labelled stress-strain curve with the six stages marked answers half the question before you write.
  • Label every point — proportional limit, elastic limit, yield point, UTS, necking, fracture.
  • State the convention — say "original area" and "original gauge length" out loud; it is worth a mark each time.
  • Underline the keywords — engineering, true, ductile, brittle, necking.
  • Do the numbers before the prose — four lines of arithmetic then a paragraph, not the reverse.
  • Never leave blank — the curve plus the six stage names clears 3 of 7.

Cheat-Sheet · BT203 Unit 1 Topic 06

Tensile Test and Stress-Strain — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

The tensile test pulls a standard specimen axially to fracture while
recording load against extension. Rescaled to stress against strain, the
curve gives the stiffness (E), the yield and ultimate strength, the
ductility and the toughness of the material in a single graph.

Derivation in four steps

1. Measure     -> load P from the load cell, extension dL from the gauge
2. Convert     -> sigma = P / A0 with A0 = (pi/4) d0^2   [ORIGINAL]
                   e = dL / L0                            [ORIGINAL gauge]
3. Plot        -> the six stages: proportional, elastic, yield,
                   strain hardening, necking, fracture
4. Read        -> E from the slope, resilience from the elastic area,
                   toughness from the total area, ductility from %EL / %RA

Reference table

QuantityFormulaUnit
Original areaA0 = π/4 × d02mm2
Area at fractureA1 = π/4 × d12mm2
Engineering stressσ = P / A0MPa = N/mm2
True stressσtrue = P / A1MPa
Engineering straine = δL / L0dimensionless
Young's modulusE = σ / e (slope of the linear part)GPa
Percentage elongation(L1 − L0)/L0 × 100%
Percentage reduction of area(A0 − A1)/A0 × 100 = (d02 − d12)/d02 × 100%
Resiliencearea under the elastic portion of the curveJ/m3
Toughnesstotal area under the curveJ/m3

Definitions to reproduce

  • Yield stress — the stress at which plastic deformation begins at practically constant load; the working limit a designer does not exceed.
  • Ultimate tensile stress — the maximum engineering stress on the curve, at the start of necking.
  • Necking — the local reduction of cross-section that begins after the ultimate point and concentrates all further deformation at one spot.
  • Engineering stress — load divided by the original cross-sectional area; used for all design work.
  • True stress — load divided by the instantaneous area; valid right up to fracture.
  • Percentage reduction of area — the ductility measure taken at the neck, normally larger than the percentage elongation for a ductile material.

Checklist before you call the topic done

  • [ ] Can draw the ductile curve with all six stages labelled, from memory
  • [ ] Can draw the brittle curve and state what is missing from it
  • [ ] Can say where Young's modulus, resilience and toughness come off the curve
  • [ ] Can distinguish engineering from true stress and say which area each uses
  • [ ] Can compute %RA from the two diameters without computing areas
  • [ ] Can explain in one sentence why engineering stress falls after UTS

Numeral shortcut

Order of operations, every time. (1) Compute A0 = π/4 d02 and A1 = π/4 d12. (2) Stress = P / A0. (3) %EL from the gauge lengths, %RA from the areas. (4) Check: %RA and %EL should be comparable and, for a ductile material, %RA should exceed %EL.

  1. Apparatus: a standard circular specimen of diameter d0 with two marked points defining the original gauge length L0, gripped in the universal testing machine between the upper and lower crossheads.
  2. Measurement: a load cell records the axial force and an extensometer or dial gauge records the extension; both are plotted to give the load-extension curve.
  3. Conversion: engineering stress σ = P / A0, where A0 = π/4 × d02, and engineering strain ε = δL / L0. Both use the original dimensions.
  4. Curve stages: proportional limit, where stress is directly proportional to strain and the slope gives Young's modulus; elastic limit, beyond which the material takes a permanent set; yield point; strain hardening up to the ultimate tensile stress; necking; and fracture.
  5. Reading the curve: resilience is the area under the elastic portion only, toughness is the area under the entire curve, and % elongation and % reduction of area at the neck give ductility.
  6. Engineering versus true: true stress uses the instantaneous area, so it keeps rising to fracture; because engineering stress uses the fixed A0, the engineering curve appears to fall after necking.

Exam close: "The curve therefore supplies every mechanical property at once — E from the slope, resilience and toughness from two different areas, and ductility from the elongation at fracture — which is why the tensile test is the single most used mechanical test in engineering."

Design consequence: a ductile material deforms visibly and necks before it breaks, so a structure made from it gives visible warning of impending failure. A brittle material has almost no plastic deformation and fractures suddenly, so failure cannot be predicted from appearance — which is why impact testing exists and why structures are designed to be ductile.

Exam close: "The area under the curve is the toughness, so the brittle material is not merely weaker but far less safe, because it offers no warning before it fails."

Exam close: "Had the necked area been used, the stress would have come out as 471 MPa — that is the true stress at fracture, not the engineering stress, and the ratio of the two is simply A0/A1."
