Lesson · BT203 Unit 1 — Materials

Classification of Engineering Materials — The Tree Every Question Starts From

Almost every BT-203 Unit 1 question begins with a classification: "classify engineering materials", "what is steel and its types", "what is cast iron and its types". If you can draw the tree, you can answer all of them. Dec-2020 asked exactly this twice — Q1a on steel and Q6a on the iron-carbon diagram. Learn the tree, and every leaf below it comes free.

The one-sentence statement

Engineering materials are classified, by the dominant metal in them, into two
branches — FERROUS (iron-based) and NON-FERROUS (no iron). Every
classification question in this unit is one of these two branches followed
by a further split.

The single most common mistake is writing "metals and non-metals". That is a periodic-table split, not an engineering split, and an examiner reading "metals and non-metals" has just watched you miss the question. Iron is a metal; copper is a metal; both sit on the same side of the ferrous/non-ferrous line. Write ferrous and non-ferrous.

The classification tree

Draw this. It is the skeleton of the whole unit, and every classification answer is a walk down one branch of it.

ENGINEERING MATERIALS
|
+-- FERROUS (iron is the base metal, >= 50% by weight)
|   |
|   +-- Carbon steels .......... Fe + 0.03% to 2.0% C
|   |   |
|   |   +-- Low carbon ......... 0.03 - 0.25% C  (mild steel, wire, sheet)
|   |   +-- Medium carbon ...... 0.25 - 0.60% C  (gears, shafts, cranks)
|   |   +-- High carbon ........ 0.60 - 1.0%  C  (tools, springs, dies)
|   |   +-- Alloy steel ......... Fe + C + Cr / Ni / Mo / V / W
|   |
|   +-- Cast irons ............. Fe + 2.0% to 6.67% C
|       |
|       +-- White cast iron .... 1.8 - 3.0% C   (very hard, highly brittle)
|       +-- Grey cast iron ..... 2.5 - 4.0% C   (flakes, dampers, beds)
|       +-- Ductile / nodular .. 3.0 - 4.0% C   (spheroidal graphite)
|       +-- Malleable cast iron . 2.5 - 3.0% C   (brittle -> ductile)
|
+-- NON-FERROUS (no iron; extracted from their own ore)
    |
    +-- Light metals ........... Al, Mg, Ti  (density < 5 g/cm3)
    +-- Heavy metals ........... Cu, Pb, Zn, Sn  (density > 5 g/cm3)
    +-- Precious ............... Au, Ag, Pt
    +-- Non-metallic ........... timber, glass, ceramics, plastics

Read the two split-points carefully, because they are the whole content of the topic:

Split 1 — iron or no iron. This divides the whole subject into two halves. Iron-based (ferrous) and everything else (non-ferrous). A piece of question text that says "materials used in..." is nearly always asking you to sort examples into these two buckets.

Split 2 — how much carbon. This is the split that matters inside ferrous metals, and it is the one that decides steel vs cast iron. Below 2.0% carbon the alloy is a steel; above 2.0% it is a cast iron. Nothing else. That one number is the boundary, and it is worth memorising exactly as 2%.

Ferrous: steel vs cast iron

The two names are not synonyms and the difference is purely carbon content. "Steel" is a word that includes every iron-carbon alloy below 2% carbon; "cast iron" is the region above it.

PropertySteelCast iron
Carbon content0.03% to 2.0%2.0% to 6.67%
Carbon formMostly combined (Fe3C)Partly free graphite
FormabilityCan be forged / welded / rolledEssentially cast only
ToughnessHighLow — brittle, no shock resistance
MachinabilityModerateGood (free graphite acts as lubricant)
Damping / vibrationPoorExcellent — absorbs noise
Typical usesGears, shafts, bolts, bridgesEngine blocks, machine beds, pipes

Two consequences of the 2% line are worth stating explicitly, because examiners reward them:

Why cast iron cannot be welded or forged: the high carbon makes the cast iron extremely brittle — brittle metals have almost no ductility, and both welding and forging require the metal to deform plastically without cracking. A brittle metal cracks before it flows. Hence cast iron is used in the cast form and is almost never wrought.
The one contradiction to flag: cast iron has excellent damping (vibration and noise absorption) and excellent machinability, yet it is brittle. These coexist because the free graphite flakes absorb vibration and lubricate the cut, but they also act as internal notches that crack under shock load. If an answer says "cast iron is hard and strong, therefore tough", it is wrong — hard and strong is not the same as tough, and cast iron is the standard counter-example.

Non-ferrous, and why we even bother

Non-ferrous metals are all non-magnetic, generally more corrosion-resistant than steel, and far lighter. The two weight classes split them:

ClassMetalsDensity (g/cm3)Signature property / use
LightAluminium, Magnesium, Titanium< 5High strength-to-weight. Aircraft, auto bodies
HeavyCopper, Lead, Zinc, Tin, Nickel> 5Electrical conduction (Cu), bearing metal (Pb)
PreciousGold, Silver, Platinum—Corrosion resistance, contacts, electrodes
Non-metallicTimber, glass, ceramics, plastics—Light, cheap, non-conductive insulators

Why non-ferrous is not simply "worse steel". Two properties no ferrous material matches cheaply:

Corrosion resistance. Stainless steel resists rust because of chromium; aluminium resists rust because of its own oxide film. Both are non-ferrous answers to the same problem. Lead and copper are used precisely for their resistance to specific acids and waters.

Damping. Cast iron is the classic ferrous damper, but the same duty in a lighter part is met by aluminium. Use the property name first, then the material.

Numeral scope in this topic

Read this before attempting any calculation. The classification of engineering materials is an entirely theoretical topic. There is no numerical question in Unit 1 that belongs to classification — every numeral in this unit belongs to the tensile test (topic 06), Hooke's law and modulus of elasticity (topic 07), or the Bernoulli-type fluid problems in Unit 3. If a BT-203 question asks you to "calculate" something about steel, cast iron, or alloy steel, it is almost certainly a stress/strain or elasticity numeral wearing a materials label. Redirect to topics 06 and 07 rather than hunting for a classification formula. There is none.
Numeral traps in this topic
  • No formula exists for "classification". Do not invent one to answer a calculate-sounding question about material types.
  • The 2% carbon line is the only number you must be exact on in this topic. Write it as a boundary with both sides: "below 2% carbon it is a steel; above 2% it is a cast iron." A one-sided answer loses the distinction mark.
  • Density boundary at 5 g/cm3 separates light from heavy non-ferrous metals. Aluminium is 2.7, copper is 8.96 — memorise those two as the proof that the split works.
Special lines for extra marks

Copy these lines directly into the answer. Each one earns a mark the question did not explicitly ask for.

  • "Engineering materials are broadly classified into ferrous (base metal iron) and non-ferrous (all other metals) materials."
  • "The dividing line between steel and cast iron is the carbon content of 2%: below it the alloy is a steel, above it a cast iron."
  • "Cast iron is brittle rather than strong, because its carbon appears partly as free graphite, which acts as internal stress concentrators."
  • "Non-ferrous metals are preferred where low density, corrosion resistance, or non-magnetic behaviour is the governing requirement, none of which ferrous grades give economically."

Close the answer by underlining the key word of the question — "ferrous and non-ferrous", "types of cast iron", "applications" — and boxing the 2% figure. Examiners scan; make the mark easy to give.

Exam tips
  • Statement first, then the tree, then examples. One line of definition, one ASCII tree, four to six worked examples with their use. That ordering alone is most of the marks.
  • Never answer "metals and non-metals". It is the wrong split and it is the single most common lost mark in this topic.
  • Write "ferrous (iron-based)" the first time. Examiners award the definition mark for saying what the word means, not for using it correctly.
  • Add the reason after every use. "Ductile cast iron is used for pipes because it resists corrosion and has shock resistance" scores a mark that a bare list of uses does not.

PYQ drill

Drill set A — theory, self-paced. Answer each in five lines or less before checking.

  1. Session tag: Dec-2020 Q1a (7 marks). "What is steel? Discuss some of the different types of steel with their applications." → Define steel as an iron-carbon alloy with 2% maximum carbon. Then the carbon ladder: low, medium, high carbon, plus alloy steel — each with its carbon range, its property, and one application. See topic 02 for the full steel answer with the alloy branch.
  2. Session tag: recurring short question. "Classify engineering materials." → The tree above, verbatim, with two examples per leaf.
  3. Session tag: recurring short question. "What is malleability? How does it differ from ductility?" → Both are plastic deformation properties. Malleability is flattening under compression (sheet, foil); ductility is drawing into a wire. Full treatment in topic 05.

Drill set B — recall under time. Write the answer, then verify.

  1. 2 minutes, no notes. Write the full ferrous/non-ferrous tree from memory. Check: did you keep 2% on the correct side?
  2. 3 minutes. Give two properties in which cast iron beats steel and one in which steel beats cast iron. Check against the comparison table above.
  3. 4 minutes. "A designer needs a machine-tool bed that damps vibration. Justify the material." → Grey cast iron — graphite flakes give high damping, and casting is cheap for a large complex shape. State the property first, then the material, then the reason.

Cheat-Sheet · BT203 Unit 1 Topic 01

Classification of Engineering Materials — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

Engineering materials are classified by their base metal into FERROUS
(iron-based) and NON-FERROUS (all other metals) materials. Ferrous
materials are then split by carbon content into carbon steels
(0.03-2.0% C) and cast irons (2.0-6.67% C).

Derivation in four steps

1. The base metal is iron        -> FERROUS branch
2. The base metal is not iron    -> NON-FERROUS branch
3. Inside ferrous, count carbon:
       carbon < 2%   -> STEEL
       carbon > 2%   -> CAST IRON
4. Inside non-ferrous, compare density against 5 g/cm3:
       density < 5    -> LIGHT metals (Al, Mg, Ti)
       density > 5    -> HEAVY metals (Cu, Pb, Zn, Sn)

Reference table

QuantityValueWhy it matters
Steel / cast iron boundary2.0% carbonDefines the two ferrous families
Carbon steel range0.03% to 2.0% CLow / medium / high bands inside it
Cast iron range2.0% to 6.67% CWhite, grey, ductile, malleable inside it
Low carbon steel0.03 - 0.25% CDuctile, weldable — sheet and wire
Medium carbon steel0.25 - 0.60% CBalance of strength and toughness — gears, shafts
High carbon steel0.60 - 1.0% CHard, wear resistant — tools, springs
Light/heavy non-ferrous line5 g/cm3Al 2.7 light, Cu 8.96 heavy

Definitions to reproduce

  • Ferrous material — an engineering material whose base metal is iron, with carbon as the primary alloying element.
  • Non-ferrous material — an engineering material with no iron content, valued for low density, corrosion resistance, or non-magnetic behaviour.
  • Cast iron — a ferrous alloy containing 2.0% to 6.67% carbon, practically unfusible (non-weldable, non-forgeable) and therefore used in the cast form.
  • Alloy steel — a steel containing added elements such as Cr, Ni, Mo, V, or W to raise strength, hardness, wear resistance, or corrosion resistance.

Checklist before you call the topic done

  • [ ] Can draw the whole tree from memory in 60 seconds
  • [ ] Can state the 2% carbon boundary with both sides
  • [ ] Can name four types of cast iron and one use each
  • [ ] Can name three types of steel and one use each
  • [ ] Can give two properties where non-ferrous beats ferrous
  • [ ] Can say why cast iron is not weldable

Numeral shortcut

There is no numeral in this topic. Classification is descriptive. If a BT-203 question appears to ask for a calculation about material types, it belongs to the tensile test (topic 06) or Hooke's law (topic 07). Redirect, do not invent a formula.

Why is cast iron preferred for machine tool beds, and why can it not be welded?

Two independent one-sided answers, each property-then-reason. First half: graphite flakes absorb vibrational energy, so the bed does not ring, plus it casts cheaply in one complex shape. write — damping property + graphite reason (2) → brittleness + plastic-deformation argument (2).

Topper answer format
  1. Statement: Give the classification rule in one precise sentence — by base metal, then by carbon.
  2. Visual: Draw the labelled ASCII classification tree, both branches, before writing any prose.
  3. Reason: One worked example per leaf — material, class, key property, application — each with a "because" clause.
  4. Close: End on the boxed boundary figure, here the 2% carbon line, underlined.

  • Tree or table before prose — the examiner scans visuals first
  • Underline keywords — ferrous, non-ferrous, mild steel, grey cast iron
  • Box every final answer — the 2% boundary, the class verdict
  • Attempt order: classification question first → tables second → explanation last

Model answer · 7 marks

Classify engineering materials. Explain the classification with suitable examples.

Engineering materials are broadly classified into ferrous and non-ferrous materials, according to whether the base metal is iron. Ferrous materials are then subdivided by carbon content into carbon steels and cast irons.

ENGINEERING MATERIALS
|
+-- FERROUS (base metal = iron)
|   |
|   +-- Carbon steel .. Fe + 0.03-2.0% C
|   |   mild ......... 0.03-0.25%  sheet, wire, beams
|   |   medium ....... 0.25-0.60%  gears, shafts, crankshafts
|   |   high ......... 0.60-1.00%  cutting tools, springs
|   |   alloy ........ + Cr/Ni/Mo   ball bearings, gears
|   |
|   +-- Cast iron ..... Fe + 2.0-6.67% C
|       white ........ 1.8-3.0% C   dies, crushers (hard, brittle)
|       grey ......... 2.5-4.0% C   beds, blocks (damping)
|       ductile ...... 3.0-4.0% C   pipes, crankshafts
|
+-- NON-FERROUS (no iron)
    light ............ Al, Mg, Ti    aircraft, conductors
    heavy ............ Cu, Pb, Zn    cables, bearings
    precious ......... Au, Ag, Pt    contacts, electrodes
  1. Ferrous: iron-based alloys. The primary alloying element is carbon, and carbon content is what splits the branch.
  2. Non-ferrous: every metal extracted from its own ore — aluminium, copper, magnesium, titanium and the rest. Valued for low density, corrosion resistance or non-magnetic behaviour.
  3. Examples: mild steel for sheet because it is ductile and weldable; medium-carbon steel for gears because it balances strength and toughness; high-carbon steel for cutting tools because it holds an edge; grey cast iron for machine beds because its graphite flakes damp vibration; copper for winding wire because it is the best conductor.
  4. Boundary: carbon content of 2% divides the ferrous branch — below it a steel, above it a cast iron.

Exam close: box the 2% boundary and end with: "The carbon content, not the presence of iron alone, decides whether a ferrous alloy is a steel or a cast iron."

Model answer · 4 marks

Why is cast iron preferred for machine tool beds, and why can it not be welded?

Two separate answers. First the material choice and its reason, then the process failure and its reason. They rest on different properties — damping versus brittleness.

              CAST IRON
                  |
          +----------------+
          |                |
      +-------+       +---------+
      |       |       |         |
      | GOOD  |       | BAD     |
      | FOR   |       | FOR     |
      | BEDS  |       | WELDING |
      |       |       |         |
      +-------+       +---------+
          |                |
   Graphite flakes    High carbon
   absorb vibration   makes it brittle
          |                |
   Damping, low       Cracks before
   ringing            plastic flow

Cast iron is preferred for machine tool beds because of its excellent damping capacity. The free graphite flakes in grey cast iron absorb vibrational energy, so the bed does not ring under cutting load; it also has high compressive strength and is cheap to cast in one complex shape.

Cast iron cannot be welded because its high carbon content makes it brittle. Welding and forging both require the metal to deform plastically without cracking; a brittle metal cracks before it flows, so the heated zone simply fractures.

Exam close: "The same graphite that damps the bed is what leaves the iron brittle, and neither property can be traded away — so a different material is chosen for each job."
