Lesson · BT203 Unit 1 — Materials

Mechanical Properties of Materials — The Pairs Examiners Ask You to Contrast

Eight properties, and almost every question about them is a contrast: ductile versus brittle, hard versus tough, malleable versus ductile, strong versus hard. If you learn each property as a definition plus a test plus a contrasting pair, this topic stops being eight separate facts and becomes four pairs you can write in any order. That is exactly how it is examined.

The one-sentence statement

Mechanical properties are those properties of a material that determine
its behaviour under applied forces. They are measured by standard tests
-- tensile, hardness and impact -- and each exists as a pair of opposites,
because raising one usually lowers its partner.

The clause that earns the extra mark is the last one. Strength and hardness, strength and toughness, ductility and strength — every pair trades against each other. Say that once and the rest of the answer is filling in a table.

The eight properties

Pair 1 — ductility versus malleability

Both are plasticity, and both are measured by permanent deformation. The difference is only the direction of the force.

DuctilityMalleability
Deformation modeDrawing into a wire — tensile forceFlattening or rolling into a sheet — compressive force
Typical measure% elongation, % reduction of areaRolling or hammering test
Best inCopper, aluminium, mild steelCopper, aluminium, lead
Least inCast iron, glass, tungstenCast iron, tungsten

Pair 2 — hardness versus toughness

The most confused pair in the unit, and the most frequently asked. Hardness is a surface property; toughness is a bulk property. A file is extremely hard and has almost no toughness; a rubber tyre is not hard at all and is enormously tough.

HardnessToughness
ResistsIndentation, scratching, abrasion, cuttingFracture under shock or gradual loading
Where it livesAt the surface, a shallow layerThrough the whole section
Measured byBrinell, Rockwell, VickersCharpy, Izod, and the area under the stress-strain curve
Can it coexist?Yes — and it is the whole aim of metallurgy: a hard surface on a tough core, as in case-hardened or nitrided parts

Pair 3 — strength versus hardness

Strength is resistance to failure under load; hardness is resistance to surface indentation. Hardness generally increases with tensile strength, but they are not the same measurement: a material can be surface-hardened without gaining bulk strength, and a strong weldment is not thereby a hard one.

Pair 4 — resilience versus toughness

Both are energy absorption, and the difference is where the load stops.

ResilienceToughness
Energy absorbedUp to the elastic limit onlyUp to fracture, through the whole curve
GraphicallyArea under the elastic portionArea under the entire stress-strain curve
Failure modeMaterial returns to its original shapeMaterial fractures
Property needed forSprings, shock absorbersConnecting rods, ships, bridges, aircraft
The one line that settles all four pairs. A spring material needs high resilience because it must store and release energy elastically without permanent set; a crankshaft needs high toughness because it must absorb a sudden load without breaking. Both absorb energy, but at different points of the curve, and that single sentence is the difference between a 4-mark and a 7-mark answer.

Given:  diameter, load (or stress) and modulus
Find:   stress, strain, or percentage elongation

Solved A — stress in a round rod

A steel rod of 10 mm diameter carries an axial load of 20 kN. Calculate the direct stress in it.

Given: d = 10 mm, P = 20 kN = 20,000 N. Find: σ. Formula: σ = P / A with A = π/4 d2. Solve:

A   = (pi / 4) x 10^2 = 78.54 mm^2
sigma = 20000 N / 78.54 mm^2 = 254.65 N/mm^2
      = 254.65 MPa

Answer: the direct stress is 254.65 MPa (about 255 MPa).

Check: units cancel to N/mm2, which is MPa by definition, so the number is a stress and not a force. Independent order-of-magnitude check: 20 kN on roughly 80 mm2 is about 250 MPa, and halving the diameter to 5 mm would cut the area to a quarter and quadruple the stress to roughly 1019 MPa — the d2 scaling is behaving correctly.

Solved B — the same stress on two different materials

A steel bar and an aluminium bar are both subjected to a stress of 140 MPa. Steel has E = 200 GPa and aluminium E = 70 GPa. Compare the strains produced.

This is the elasticity property doing its job: for a fixed stress, the stiffer material deforms less.

Given: σ = 140 MPa, Es = 200 GPa = 200,000 MPa, Eal = 70 GPa = 70,000 MPa. Find: strain in each. Formula: ε = σ / E. Solve:

steel:      e = 140 / 200000 = 0.000700
aluminium:  e = 140 /  70000 = 0.002000

Answer: steel strains 0.0007, aluminium strains 0.0020. Aluminium deforms about 2.86 times as much, so steel is the stiffer of the two.

Check: the ratio must equal the ratio of the moduli, because the stress cancels: 200,000 / 70,000 = 2.8571, and 0.0020 / 0.0007 = 2.8571 as well. Two routes, same figure. Note also that 0.0007 strain is 0.7 mm on a 1 m bar — small, as elastic strains should be. If your answer comes out in metres rather than as a pure number, the modulus was left in GPa instead of being converted to MPa.

Solved C — ductility from two gauge lengths

A specimen of ductile metal has an original gauge length of 50 mm. After fracture it measures 62 mm. Calculate the percentage elongation and comment on its ductility.

Given: L0 = 50 mm, L1 = 62 mm. Find: % elongation. Formula: (L1 − L0) / L0 × 100. Solve:

% elongation = (62 - 50) / 50 x 100 = 12 / 50 x 100 = 24%

Answer: 24%. For a ferrous metal, a value of 20% or more is regarded as high ductility — compare with a cast iron, typically under 1% — so this specimen is a ductile metal and will neck noticeably before fracture.

Check: the elongation itself is 12 mm on a 50 mm gauge, a ratio of 12/50 = 0.24, so 24% follows. Independent sanity check: the gauge length must be measured after joining the two fractured halves, and the increase can only come from plastic elongation, never from elastic recovery — elastic recovery on unloading reduces the length, so the true plastic elongation is at least as large as the 24% computed. The order of magnitude is right for mild steel.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • MPa versus GPa. If E is given as 200 GPa and stress in MPa, convert E to 200,000 MPa or convert stress to GPa. Mixing them puts the strain three orders of magnitude out.
  • Use the original area, not the necked area. Stress is force divided by the original cross-section. Topic 06 covers the true-stress case separately.
  • A = π/4 d2, not π d2 and not π/2 d2. The factor of four is the single most common arithmetic slip in these questions.
  • Percentage elongation is relative to the original gauge length — L0 is always the denominator, never L1.
  • Hardness and toughness are not interchangeable. If a question asks which is higher for a file versus a tyre, the answer is hardness for the file and toughness for the tyre; swapping them is the standard wrong answer.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Mechanical properties are those properties of a material that determine its behaviour under externally applied forces, and each is measured by a standard mechanical test.”
  • Significance line — “These properties occur in opposing pairs — hardness against toughness, strength against ductility — so raising one generally costs its partner, and metallurgy is largely the art of arranging that trade in the right direction.”
  • Method line — “Each property is stated as definition, test, and contrasting partner, which is the order the examiner awards marks in.”
  • Limitation line — “Hardness tests are surface tests, so a hardened case gives a high reading while the core underneath remains soft; hardness alone therefore says nothing about toughness.”
  • Closing line — “Hence a material is judged by the pair it satisfies — resilience for a spring, toughness for a crankshaft — and no single property qualifies a material on its own.”
Exam tips
  • Definition, test, contrast — in that order — it maps directly onto the mark allocation.
  • Table before prose — eight rows of property, definition and test is the answer.
  • Always give the contrasting partner — an unpaired definition loses the distinction mark, which is usually the one being tested.
  • Underline the keyword — ductile, brittle, malleable, resilience, toughness.
  • Attempt order — this question is pure recall; take it before any calculation.
  • Never leave blank — four properties with their pairs clears 4 of 7.

Cheat-Sheet · BT203 Unit 1 Topic 05

Mechanical Properties — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

Mechanical properties determine how a material behaves under applied
forces. Each is measured by a standard test and each exists as a pair of
opposites, because raising one generally lowers its partner: hardness
against toughness, strength against ductility.

Derivation in four steps

1. Name the property  -> elasticity, ductility, malleability, hardness,
                            toughness, resilience, brittleness, strength
2. State the mechanism  -> e.g. elasticity = returns to original shape
3. Name the test        -> tensile / Brinell / Rockwell / Izod / Charpy
4. Name the CONTRASTING PARTNER -> the mark that is actually being tested

Reference table

PropertyMeansTestContrast
ElasticityRegains original shape on unloadingE = stress / strainvs plasticity
PlasticityDeforms permanently without rupture% elongation, % RAvs elasticity
DuctilityDrawn into a wire (tension)% elongation, % reduction of areavs malleability
MalleabilityFlattened into a sheet (compression)Rolling or hammering testvs ductility
BrittlenessFractures with almost no plastic flowIzod / Charpy impactvs ductility
HardnessResists indentation and abrasion (surface)Brinell, Rockwell, Vickersvs toughness
ToughnessAbsorbs energy to fracture (bulk)Charpy / Izod, area under curvevs hardness, vs resilience
ResilienceAbsorbs energy in the elastic range onlyArea under elastic portionvs toughness
StrengthResists load without breakingTensile test, yield / UTSvs ductility

Definitions to reproduce

  • Ductility — the ability to undergo plastic deformation in tension, as in drawing into wire; measured by percentage elongation and reduction of area.
  • Malleability — the ability to undergo plastic deformation in compression, as in rolling into sheet.
  • Brittleness — the property of fracturing with little or no plastic deformation; the opposite of ductility.
  • Hardness — resistance to surface indentation, scratching or abrasion; measured by Brinell, Rockwell or Vickers.
  • Toughness — the ability to absorb energy up to fracture, equal to the area under the whole stress-strain curve.
  • Resilience — the ability to absorb energy within the elastic limit; the area under the elastic portion of the curve.

Checklist before you call the topic done

  • [ ] Can name all eight properties with a test for each
  • [ ] Can state ductility versus malleability as tension versus compression
  • [ ] Can state hardness versus toughness as surface versus bulk
  • [ ] Can explain resilience versus toughness as two areas of the same curve
  • [ ] Can give one application for resilience and one for toughness
  • [ ] Can convert between GPa and MPa inside a strain calculation

Numeral shortcut

Find the area before anything else. A = π/4 d2. Then σ = P / A on the original area, and ε = σ / E with E in the same units as σ. For percentage elongation the original gauge length is always the denominator. Check the strain comes out as a pure number.

Differentiate between hardness and toughness. Can a material be both?

AspectHardnessToughness
MeaningResistance to indentation, scratching, abrasion or cuttingAbility to absorb energy and resist fracture
Where it actsAt the surface, in a shallow layerThrough the whole section
TestBrinell, Rockwell, VickersCharpy, Izod, or the area under the stress-strain curve

Yes, a material can be both. Surface hardening — carburising, nitriding, induction hardening or case hardening — produces a hard, wear-resistant outer layer while the core underneath stays tough. That combination is why gears, crankshafts and railway axles are case-hardened: the surface takes the wear, and the core absorbs the shock without cracking.

Exam close: "Hardness and toughness are not opposites but different kinds of resistance — one at the surface to abrasion, one through the bulk to fracture — and combining them in one part is the central problem of surface engineering."

Model answer · 5 marks

What is resilience? How does it differ from toughness, and where is each required?

Both are energy absorption. Say that first, then split them by where the load stops, then give one application each.

            STRESS
              |                       FRACTURE
              |            +---------x
              |          / |  toughness: area under
              |        /   |  the WHOLE curve
              |      /  P  |
              |    /    /|  +------------------------+
              |  /   E/  |  | resilience: area under  |
              |/   /     |  | the ELASTIC portion only |
              +-----------+  +------------------------+
              |    |
              +----+--> STRAIN

   SPRING: stores energy, returns to shape -> RESILIENCE
   CRANKSHAFT: absorbs a sudden load       -> TOUGHNESS

Resilience is the ability of a material to absorb energy when it is deformed within the elastic limit and to return to its original shape on unloading. Graphically it is the area under the elastic portion of the stress-strain curve.

Toughness is the ability to absorb energy up to fracture, so it is the area under the entire stress-strain curve. The two are therefore the same idea read off different parts of one graph: resilience stops at the elastic limit, toughness carries on to the point of fracture.

Applications: resilience is required in springs and shock absorbers, which must store and release energy elastically without taking a permanent set. Toughness is required in connecting rods, crankshafts, ship hulls and bridges, which must absorb a sudden or shock load without fracturing.

Exam close: "A spring material is chosen for resilience and a crankshaft for toughness, so selecting the property means deciding whether the energy must be returned or merely absorbed."
