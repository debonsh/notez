Lesson · BT203 Unit 1 — Materials

Cast Iron and Carbon Steels — Composition and Types

This is Dec-2020 Q1a, asked almost verbatim: "What is steel? Discuss some of the different types of steel with their applications." It is the highest-scoring recall question in Unit 1, because the whole answer is four carbon bands and four cast-iron types. There is one idea doing all the work — carbon decides everything — and once you own that, the tables write themselves.

The one-sentence statement

A carbon steel is an iron-carbon alloy containing 0.03% to 2.0% carbon; a cast
iron is the same alloy system above 2.0% carbon, up to 6.67%. Raising
carbon raises hardness and strength but destroys ductility and
weldability, so each band ends up with its own application.

Write that sentence before any table. It carries the definition mark, and the last clause (“raising carbon…”) is the sentence that connects every row you are about to write.

Two of those figures carry more marks than the rest. 2.0% is the steel / cast-iron boundary, and 0.77% is the eutectoid carbon content at 727°C, which decides whether a steel is hypo- or hypereutectoid. You meet 0.77% again in topic 03.

Write these as a table, not prose. Four rows with four columns is roughly a third of the time a seven-mark question costs you, and an examiner reads a table in ten seconds where a paragraph takes a minute.

The four cast irons

The whole family turns on one question: what form does the carbon take? If it is combined with iron as cementite (Fe3C) the iron is white and extremely brittle; if it separates out as free graphite, the iron becomes grey, softer and far less brittle.

The distinction examiners actually test. White versus grey is not a colour difference and not a carbon-percentage difference — both can sit near 3%. It is free graphite present or absent. A cast iron with flakes is grey; one with the carbon tied up as Fe3C is white. Saying “white cast iron contains more carbon” loses the mark.

Numericals in great depth

This topic is mostly recall, but three things about it do get calculated, and all three are composition arithmetic on a mass basis. Learn the identifier table, learn the format, and these stop being hard.

Five-line answer format — use this for every composition numeral 
Given:  mass and the analysis (percentages by mass)
Find:   the quantity actually asked for
Formula:m_e = m x p / 100
Solve:  substitute, one step, write the unit
Check:  add the parts back to the total, or divide back to get p

Solved A — identify the material and recover the iron mass

A batch of cast iron weighs 250 kg and analyses 3.2% carbon and 2.5% silicon, the balance being iron. (i) Confirm whether it is a steel or a cast iron. (ii) Find the mass of iron in the batch. (iii) Name the type and one application.

Given: m = 250 kg, pC = 3.2%, pSi = 2.5%. Find: iron mass. Formula: me = m × p / 100. Solve:

p_Fe = 100 - (3.2 + 2.5) = 94.3%
m_Fe = 250 x 94.3 / 100 = 235.75 kg
m_C  = 250 x  3.2 / 100 =   8.00 kg
m_Si = 250 x  2.5 / 100 =   6.25 kg

Answer: 3.2% carbon is above the 2% line, so it is a cast iron, not a steel. Iron mass = 235.75 kg. With 2.5% silicon acting as a graphitiser it is a grey cast iron, used for machine tool beds and engine blocks.

Check: add the three masses back — 235.75 + 8.00 + 6.25 = 250.00 kg, exactly the batch weight. Then divide back: 8.00 / 250 × 100 = 3.2%, the stated carbon. Two independent returns to the given data, so the arithmetic is sound rather than merely plausible.

Solved B — mass of carbon in a heat of steel

A heat of 500 kg of steel contains 0.35% carbon. Find the mass of carbon and the mass of iron, and identify the steel.

Given: m = 500 kg, pC = 0.35%. Find: mC, mFe. Formula: me = m × p / 100. Solve:

m_C  = 500 x 0.35 / 100 = 1.75 kg
m_Fe = 500 - 1.75       = 498.25 kg   (or 500 x 99.65 / 100)

Answer: carbon = 1.75 kg, iron = 498.25 kg. 0.35% falls in the 0.25–0.60% band, so this is a medium carbon steel, used for gears, shafts and crankshafts.

Check: 1.75 + 498.25 = 500.00 kg, and 1.75 / 500 × 100 = 0.35% — the input value returns. Independently, note that the carbon mass is a suspiciously small fraction, 1.75 / 500 ≈ 0.0035, and 0.0035 × 100 = 0.35%, which is the definition of percent. The scale is right.

Solved C — carbon to add, where the denominator changes

How much carbon must be added to 200 kg of pure iron to produce a steel containing 0.6% carbon?

This one is the classic trap: the percentage is taken over the final mass, not the initial one.

Given: mFe = 200 kg, target pC = 0.6%. Find: x, the carbon to add. Formula: x / (mFe + x) = p / 100. Solve:

x / (200 + x) = 0.006
x = 0.006 (200 + x) = 1.2 + 0.006 x
x (1 - 0.006) = 1.2
x = 1.2 / 0.994 = 1.2072 kg

Answer: add 1.21 kg of carbon; the resulting steel weighs 201.21 kg.

Check: substitute back — 1.2072 / 201.2072 × 100 = 0.600%, exactly the target. Second, independent check by ratio: in a 0.6% steel the carbon-to-iron ratio is 0.6 : 99.4, so 200 kg of iron needs 200 × 0.6 / 99.4 = 1.2072 kg of carbon. Two routes, same answer to four figures. The naive “1.2 kg” would give 1.2 / 201.2 = 0.5964%, which is why the algebra matters.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • The denominator moves. For “carbon to add” the percentage is over the final mass. Answering 0.6% × 200 = 1.2 kg gives 0.5964%, not 0.6%.
  • 2.0% is a ceiling, not a midpoint. Steel is 0.03–2.0% carbon; a steel at 1.9% is still a steel. Writing “steel contains 2% carbon” without the word maximum reads as a single point value.
  • Percent by mass, always. Carbon content in steel is mass percent. Never attempt a volume or mole fraction on a cast-iron analysis.
  • Do not classify white versus grey by colour or by carbon level. The test is free graphite present or absent. Both types sit near 3% carbon.
  • Silicon and manganese are not decoration. In real cast iron, silicon is a graphitiser and manganese a stabiliser; if the question gives you Si, it usually wants the grey-versus-white reasoning, not just a subtraction.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Carbon steels and cast irons are the same iron–carbon alloy system; only the carbon content separates them, at 2.0%.”
  • Significance line — “Carbon strengthens iron by forming cementite and pearlite, so hardness and tensile strength rise with carbon while ductility and weldability fall.”
  • Method line — “Every composition figure is taken as mass percent, so the mass of one element is the total mass multiplied by its percentage over 100.”
  • Limitation line — “These bands are engineering classifications; a real steel also contains manganese, silicon, phosphorus and sulphur, and it is these residuals, not carbon alone, that fix the weldability limit.”
  • Closing line — “Hence the family divides at 2.0% carbon, and every row above is an application chosen to exploit the property that carbon has taken away.”
Exam tips
  • Statement first — one line defining both families before either table appears.
  • Table before prose — the examiner scans a four-row table in ten seconds and a four-paragraph essay in a minute.
  • Underline the keyword — steel, cast iron, carbon, free graphite, application.
  • Every application needs its reason — “gears, because medium carbon balances strength and toughness” scores the mark a bare list does not.
  • Attempt order — this question first on the paper; it is pure recall and banks 7 marks in 12 minutes.
  • Never leave a blank — write the definition and one row per type, then close. Even partial answers clear 3 of 7.

Cheat-Sheet · BT203 Unit 1 Topic 02

Cast Iron and Carbon Steels — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

A carbon steel is an iron-carbon alloy with 0.03-2.0% carbon; a cast
iron is the same system from 2.0% up to 6.67% carbon. Carbon raises
hardness and strength and destroys ductility and weldability, so
each carbon band has its own application.

Derivation in four steps

1. Name the system      -> iron + carbon (silicon, manganese as residuals)
2. Place the carbon      -> below 2.0%  = STEEL
                            above 2.0%  = CAST IRON (max 6.67%)
3. Inside cast iron, ask  -> carbon as cementite Fe3C  = WHITE (hard, brittle)
                             carbon as free graphite   = GREY (flakes)
                             spheroids after Mg        = DUCTILE / nodular
                             rosettes after heat treat = MALLEABLE
4. Inside steel, ladder  -> 0.03-0.25 low, 0.25-0.60 medium, 0.60-1.0 high,
                            0.77% = eutectoid, >0.77% = hypereutectoid

Reference table

QuantityValueWhy it matters
Steel range0.03 - 2.0% CDefines the carbon-steel family
Cast iron range2.0 - 6.67% CWhite, grey, ductile, malleable inside it
Low carbon (mild)0.03 - 0.25% CDuctile, weldable — sheet and wire
Medium carbon0.25 - 0.60% CBalance of strength and toughness — gears
High carbon0.60 - 1.00% CHard and wear resistant — tools, springs

Definitions to reproduce

  • Carbon steel — an iron-carbon alloy containing 0.03% to 2.0% carbon, with no other significant alloying addition.
  • Cast iron — an iron-carbon alloy containing more than 2.0% carbon, up to 6.67%, so brittle that it must be used in the cast form.
  • Cementite — the intermetallic compound Fe3C; hard and brittle, and the reason white cast iron is unmachinable by ordinary means.
  • Graphite — free carbon in cast iron; as flakes it gives grey iron, as spheroids ductile iron, as rosettes malleable iron.
  • Alloy steel — a steel with added Cr, Ni, Mo, V or W to raise strength, hardness, wear resistance or corrosion resistance beyond what carbon alone can give.

Checklist before you call the topic done

  • [ ] Can state both definitions with their exact carbon ranges
  • [ ] Can reproduce the four-carbon-band table with exact ranges
  • [ ] Can name four cast irons and separate them by carbon form
  • [ ] Can say why cast iron is not weldable, in one sentence with a reason
  • [ ] Can give one application with its reason for each of the four steels
  • [ ] Can solve “carbon to add” with the denominator on the final mass

Numeral shortcut

Three composition arithmetic moves cover the whole topic. Mass of one element = total mass × its percent / 100. Iron percent = 100 minus every stated percent. Carbon to add: solve x / (m + x) = p / 100, then check by dividing x back by (m + x). Always finish with the parts summing to the total.

Differentiate between steel and cast iron, and explain why cast iron cannot be welded.

Cast iron cannot be welded because its high carbon content makes it brittle. Welding and forging both require the metal to deform plastically without cracking; a brittle metal cracks before it flows, so the heated zone fractures instead of fusing.

Exam close: "The same brittleness that makes cast iron an excellent damping material makes it unweldable — one property, two opposite consequences."
