Lesson · BT204 Unit 1 — Building Materials & Construction

Building Materials — Stones, Bricks, Cement and the Workability of Concrete

This is the biggest unit in BT-204 and the one with the most repeated question in the whole subject: “Explain the workability of concrete and the factors affecting it” has appeared in 6 of the last 10 papers. Stones (3), brick bonds (4), brick tests (3) and cement (3) rotate around it. Everything here is definition-and-property work with a handful of quantity numerals, so the marks come from naming the right properties and the right tests.

The one-sentence statement

Building materials are the materials used in the construction of a structure,
classified as natural (stone, timber, lime, clay) and artificial (bricks,
cement, steel, concrete, glass). The three that carry the marks are stone --
chosen for strength, durability and appearance; brick -- a burnt clay unit of
standard size, tested for compressive strength, water absorption and
efflorescence; and cement -- a calcareous and argillaceous mixture burnt to
clinker and ground to a powder, which sets and hardens by hydration. Concrete
is cement, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate and water, and its WORKABILITY is
the property that determines how easily the fresh mix can be mixed, placed,
compacted and finished without segregation or bleeding.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Every one of these materials is judged by the same three questions — what is it made of, what does it do well, and how do you test that it does it?”

The properties a question asks for: strength, hardness, durability, porosity and the appearance that follows from them. The five standard laboratory tests on stone are:

TestWhat it measures
Crushing testCompressive strength — the single most important property for a structural stone
Water absorption testPorosity, and hence durability: a stone absorbing more than about 5% of its dry weight is suspect
Attrition testRate of wear — for road stone
Impact testToughness — resistance to sudden shock
Acid testWhether the stone is calcareous (attacked by acid, so unfit near a coast or in polluted air)
Granite against laterite, in one line. Granite is hard, heavy, non-porous and costly. Laterite is soft, light, porous and cheap, and it hardens on exposure. The examiner almost always wants the contrast rather than either definition alone.

Brick — standard size, properties and tests

A brick is a burnt clay unit. Its standard nominal size is 200 × 100 × 100 mm with the mortar joint, that is a 190 × 90 × 90 mm brick plus a 10 mm joint.

PropertyWhat good brick should have
ColourUniform, deep red or cherry — a pale or bloated colour means under-burning
Shape and sizeSharp edges, square corners, flat faces, uniform size
StrengthCompressive strength of a first-class brick is at least about 10 N/mm2
Water absorptionFirst-class brick should absorb not more than about 20% of its weight; 12–15% is good
SoundnessA clear metallic ringing when struck — a dull thud means a crack
EfflorescenceNone. White patches after soaking mean soluble salts, which will spoil the plaster
Field testWhat it shows
Water absorptionSoak for 24 hours and weigh; the gain gives the porosity
Compressive strengthTest on a mortar-capped specimen; gives the structural class
EfflorescenceSoak and dry repeatedly; visible salt deposit means rejection
SoundnessTwo bricks struck together should ring clearly
Hardness and impactA scratch with a fingernail should leave no mark

Cement — composition and setting

Portland cement is made by burning a calcareous material (limestone or chalk) with an argillaceous one (clay or shale) to a clinker and grinding it with a little gypsum.

IngredientSourceFunction
Lime (CaO)Limestone, chalkGives strength and soundness
Silica (SiO2)Clay, shaleGives the strength that develops on setting
Alumina (Al2O3)Clay, shaleGives quick setting — too much weakens the cement
Gypsum (CaSO4)Added at grindingRetards setting so the cement can be worked
Setting stageMeaningTypical time
Initial setting timeThe time from adding water until the paste begins to stiffen and loses its plasticity — the window in which it may still be placed and compactedNot less than 30 minutes
Final setting timeThe time until the paste has hardened completely and will bear loadNot more than 10 hours (600 minutes)
The gypsum mark. Gypsum is added to retard the setting — that is, to make it slower. Without it, the cement would flash-set as soon as water touched it and could never be placed. Candidates who say “gypsum accelerates setting” lose the mark, and it is asked often because the answer is counter-intuitive.

Concrete and the workability of concrete

Concrete is cement, fine aggregate (sand), coarse aggregate (crushed stone or gravel) and water. Its two properties in the fresh state and the hardened state are examined separately.

Workability is the property that determines how easily fresh concrete can be mixed, placed, compacted and finished without segregation or bleeding. That is the definition the 6-of-10-papers question asks for.

Factor affecting workabilityHow it acts
Water contentMore water means more workable but less strong — the central trade-off of concrete
Aggregate shapeRounded and smooth particles flow more easily than angular or flaky ones
Aggregate gradingWell-graded (mixed sizes) fills voids and needs less paste, so it is more workable than single-size aggregate
Aggregate/cement ratioMore aggregate per unit of paste means a harsher, less workable mix
Cement contentMore cement paste lubricates the mix
AdmixturesPlasticisers and air-entraining agents raise workability without adding water
Time since mixingWorkability falls with time as hydration begins and water evaporates
TemperatureHot weather accelerates hydration and water loss, so workability drops faster
Workability testWhat it measures
Slump testThe fall of a moulded concrete cone when the mould is lifted — the commonest site test
Compacting factor testThe ratio of the weight of concrete actually compacted to the weight if fully compacted — the laboratory test for low-workability mixes
Vee-Bee consistometerThe time for a cone of concrete to remould into a cylinder under vibration
Flow testThe spread of a concrete disc under a standard number of jolts

Solved A — number of bricks for a wall

A brick wall is 10 m long, 3 m high and 230 mm thick. Find the number of bricks required, allowing 5% for wastage. Take a brick with its mortar joint as 200 × 100 × 100 mm.

Given: L = 10 m, H = 3 m, t = 230 mm = 0.23 m; brick with mortar 200 × 100 × 100 mm; wastage 5%. Find: the number of bricks. Formula: V = LHt; v = 0.20 × 0.10 × 0.10; N = V/v, then add wastage. Solve:

V = 10 x 3 x 0.23 = 6.90 m3

v = 0.20 x 0.10 x 0.10 = 0.002 m3

N = 6.90 / 0.002 = 3450 bricks

with 5% wastage:  3450 x 1.05 = 3622.5
                  ~ 3623 bricks

Answer: 3450 bricks are needed before wastage, and 3623 should be ordered.

Check: substituting back, 3450 × 0.002 = 6.90 m3, exactly the wall volume, so the division is consistent. Independent magnitude check: 3450 bricks in 6.9 m3 is about 500 bricks per cubic metre, which is the standard figure a mason carries. Note the commonest error: using the actual brick size of 190 × 90 × 90 mm instead of the nominal size with the mortar joint. That gives 0.001539 m3 and 4483 bricks — about 30% too many, because it forgets the 10 mm of mortar the brick does not occupy.

Solved B — cement content of a concrete mix

Find the quantity of cement required for 1 m3 of M20 concrete in the proportion 1 : 1.5 : 3 by volume, taking the dry volume factor as 1.52 and the bulk density of cement as 1440 kg/m3.

Given: Vwet = 1 m3; mix 1 : 1.5 : 3; dry volume factor 1.52; ρc = 1440 kg/m3. Find: the mass of cement. Formula: Vdry = 1.52 Vwet; Vc = Vdry / Σparts; C = Vc ρc. Solve:

V_dry = 1.52 x 1 = 1.52 m3

sum of parts = 1 + 1.5 + 3 = 5.5

V_c = 1.52 / 5.5 = 0.2764 m3

C = 0.2764 x 1440 = 398.0 kg

Answer: the cement required is 398 kg, that is about 8 bags of 50 kg.

Check: substituting back, 398/1440 = 0.2764 m3, and 0.2764 × 5.5 = 1.52 m3, recovering the dry volume. The independent check is the one worth carrying: M20 concrete takes about 400 kg of cement per cubic metre, and 398 is within 0.5% of that. Had the dry volume factor been omitted, the answer would have been 262 kg, which is well below any M20 specification and would be caught immediately by that same anchor.

Solved C — water content and the water-cement ratio

The M20 mix above is to be made with a water-cement ratio of 0.5. Find the quantity of water required, and state what happens to the strength and to the workability if the ratio is raised to 0.6.

Given: cement C = 398.0 kg; w/c = 0.5. Find: the water, and the effect of raising the ratio. Formula: w = (w/c) × C. Solve:

w = 0.5 x 398.0 = 199.0 kg  = 199 litres

  (1 litre of water weighs 1 kg, so the mass figure is also
   the volume -- a useful shortcut in every concrete numeral)

if w/c rises to 0.6:
   w = 0.6 x 398.0 = 238.8 kg
   extra water = 39.8 kg per m3 of concrete

Answer: the water required is 199 kg, that is 199 litres. Raising the ratio to 0.6 adds about 40 litres per cubic metre.

Check: substituting back, 199.0/398.0 = 0.5, recovering the stated ratio. Independent sense check: 199 litres of water in 1 m3 of concrete is about a fifth of the volume, which is the right order for a plastic mix. On the effect: raising the water-cement ratio increases workability, because the extra water lubricates the mix and the particles slide past one another more easily. It decreases strength, because the surplus water occupies space that would otherwise be filled by hydration products, and it leaves capillary voids as it evaporates. That single trade-off is what the workability question is really testing, and the usual figure quoted is that strength falls roughly 10% for every 0.05 rise in the ratio beyond the optimum.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Use the nominal brick size with the mortar joint — 200 × 100 × 100 mm, not the 190 × 90 × 90 mm the brick is actually made at. Using the moulded size overstates the brick count by about 30%.
  • Keep every dimension in metres. A wall thickness given as 230 mm is 0.23 m; a brick dimension given in mm must be converted before multiplying.
  • The dry volume factor is about 1.52 for concrete (1.33 for mortar). Omitting it understates the cement by a third.
  • Divide by the SUM of the parts, not by the cement part alone. For 1 : 1.5 : 3 the divisor is 5.5.
  • 1 litre of water weighs 1 kg, so the mass and volume figures for water are the same number. Do not convert twice.
  • Wastage is added on top, not deducted: multiply by 1.05 for 5%, never by 0.95.
  • More water means more workable AND weaker. Either half of that statement alone is wrong, and the pair is what the question is scored on.
  • Water absorption of a good brick is under about 20%, and of a good stone under about 5%. The two figures are different and are easily swapped.
  • Gypsum retards setting. It is not an accelerator, however much it sounds like one.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Building materials are those materials which are used for the construction of a structure, and they are broadly classified into natural materials such as stone, timber, clay and lime, and artificial materials such as bricks, cement, concrete, glass and steel.”
  • Workability line — “Workability of concrete is that property which determines the ease with which the fresh concrete can be mixed, placed, compacted and finished without segregation or bleeding.”
  • Factors line — “Workability depends upon the water content, the shape and grading of the aggregate, the aggregate-cement ratio, the cement content, any admixture used, the time since mixing and the temperature.”
  • Trade-off line — “An increase in the water content increases the workability but decreases the strength and durability, because the surplus water leaves capillary voids on evaporation, so the water-cement ratio is the central compromise in concrete design.”
  • Stone line — “The principal properties of a good building stone are strength, hardness, durability, and a low porosity; the laboratory tests used to establish them are the crushing test, the water absorption test, the attrition test, the impact test and the acid test.”
  • Closing line — “Hence the selection of a building material is governed by the property that the particular element demands — strength for a structural member, and durability and appearance for a face exposed to weather.”
Exam tips
  • Workability first — it is the most-repeated question in the subject, and it is a definition plus a list of factors
  • Give the factors as a table with the direction of the effect, not a bare list
  • Always pair the trade-off — water up, workability up, strength down
  • Name the tests for whichever material is asked about; a property with no test scores half
  • Draw one diagram — the classification tree, or a labelled brick with its nominal size marked
  • Underline keywords — segregation, bleeding, calcareous, argillaceous, hydration, efflorescence
  • Never leave blank — the definition plus four factors clears 4 of 7

Definitions to reproduce

  • Workability of concrete — the property determining the ease with which fresh concrete can be mixed, placed, compacted and finished without segregation or bleeding.
  • Segregation — the separation of the coarse aggregate from the mortar, leaving a honeycombed and weak concrete.
  • Bleeding — the rise of water to the surface of freshly placed concrete, which weakens the top layer.
  • Slump test — the workability test measuring the fall of a moulded cone of concrete when the mould is lifted.
  • Compacting factor — the ratio of the weight of concrete actually compacted to the weight it would have if fully compacted.
  • Calcareous material — one containing lime, such as limestone or chalk; in cement manufacture it supplies the CaO.
  • Argillaceous material — one containing clay or shale; in cement manufacture it supplies the silica and alumina.
  • Initial setting time — the time from adding water until the paste begins to stiffen and lose plasticity; not less than 30 minutes.
  • Final setting time — the time until the paste has hardened completely and will bear load; not more than 10 hours.
  • Efflorescence — a white salt deposit appearing on the surface of bricks or plaster as soluble salts migrate and crystallise.
  • Bulking of sand — the increase in the volume of moist sand due to films of water around the particles, which must be allowed for in volume batching.

Numeral shortcut

Three relations. Brickwork: volume of the wall divided by 0.002 m3 per brick with its mortar joint, then add wastage. Concrete: dry volume = 1.52 × wet, cement volume = dry divided by the sum of the parts, mass = volume × 1440 — and M20 should land near 400 kg/m3, which is the anchor that catches every slip. Water: mass = ratio × cement, and 1 litre weighs 1 kg. Keep everything in metres.

Definition. Workability of concrete is that property which determines the ease with which the fresh concrete can be mixed, placed, compacted and finished without segregation or bleeding. Segregation is the separation of the coarse aggregate from the mortar, and bleeding is the rise of water to the surface; both leave a honeycombed and weak concrete.

FactorHow it affects workability
Water contentMore water gives greater workability — the dominant factor
Aggregate shapeRounded and smooth particles are more workable than angular, flaky or elongated ones
Aggregate gradingWell-graded aggregate, with mixed sizes, fills voids and is more workable than single-size aggregate
Aggregate/cement ratioA higher ratio, that is more aggregate per unit of paste, makes the mix harsher and less workable
Cement contentMore cement paste lubricates the mix and raises workability
AdmixturesPlasticisers and air-entraining agents raise workability without adding water
Time since mixingWorkability falls with time as hydration begins and water evaporates
TemperatureHot weather accelerates hydration and evaporation, so workability falls faster

The trade-off, stated as a pair. An increase in water content increases the workability, because the extra water lubricates the particles so they slide past one another more easily. At the same time it decreases the strength and durability, because the surplus water occupies space that hydration products would otherwise fill and leaves capillary voids when it evaporates. It also increases shrinkage and the risk of bleeding. The usual figure quoted is that the strength falls by roughly 10% for every 0.05 increase in the water-cement ratio beyond the optimum, which is why the ratio is the central compromise of mix design.

Exam close: “Hence workability and strength pull in opposite directions with respect to water, so a concrete mix is designed to have just enough water to be placed and compacted properly and no more — which is why admixtures are used when further workability is needed without adding water.”

The properties that matter. Strength, hardness, durability, porosity, and the appearance that follows from the surface finish. Porosity is the one that governs the others: a porous stone absorbs water, water freezes and expands in cold weather, and the stone splits — which is why the water absorption test is the durability test.

Laboratory testWhat it establishes
Crushing testCompressive strength — the most important structural property
Water absorption testPorosity and hence durability; a good stone absorbs under about 5% of its dry weight
Attrition testRate of wear — for road stone
Impact testToughness, the resistance to sudden shock
Acid testWhether the stone is calcareous, and therefore unfit near a coast or in polluted air

Exam close: “Hence the choice of stone is governed by where it will be used — a hard non-porous stone such as granite for a structural or exposed position, and a cheap porous one such as laterite for a boundary wall — and each of those properties is established by a specific laboratory test.”

Model answer · 5 marks

A brick wall is 10 m long, 3 m high and 230 mm thick. Find the number of bricks, allowing 5% for wastage. Find also the cement for 1 m³ of M20 concrete in the proportion 1 : 1.5 : 3.

Two numerals, each with one factor that must not be forgotten: the mortar joint for the bricks and the dry volume factor for the concrete.

   PART 1 - BRICKWORK                  PART 2 - CONCRETE
   ==================                  =================

   wall 10 x 3 x 0.23                  M20 = 1 : 1.5 : 3
        = 6.90 m3                      sum of parts = 5.5

   brick WITH MORTAR JOINTS            Vdry = 1.52 x 1
     200 x 100 x 100 mm                     = 1.52 m3
        = 0.20 x 0.10 x 0.10
        = 0.002 m3                     Vcement = 1.52 / 5.5
                                                = 0.2764 m3
   N = 6.90 / 0.002
     = 3450 bricks                    C = 0.2764 x 1440
                                          = 398.0 kg
   +5% wastage
     = 3450 x 1.05                     ANCHOR: M20 needs about
     = 3622.5  ~ 3623 bricks                 400 kg per m3
                                          -> 398 confirms it

   THE MORTAR JOINT is the 10 mm the brick does NOT occupy.
   Using the moulded 190 x 90 x 90 mm gives 4483 bricks -- 30% too many.

   THE DRY VOLUME FACTOR 1.52 accounts for the voids in the aggregate.
   Omitting it gives 262 kg -- below any M20 specification.

Part one — brickwork. Given: L = 10 m, H = 3 m, t = 230 mm = 0.23 m; nominal brick 200 × 100 × 100 mm; wastage 5%. Find: the number of bricks. Formula: V = LHt; v = 0.20 × 0.10 × 0.10; N = V/v. Solve:

V = 10 x 3 x 0.23 = 6.90 m3
v = 0.20 x 0.10 x 0.10 = 0.002 m3
N = 6.90 / 0.002 = 3450 bricks
with 5% wastage = 3450 x 1.05 = 3623 bricks

Part two — cement content. Given: 1 m3 of M20, mix 1 : 1.5 : 3, dry factor 1.52, ρc = 1440 kg/m3. Find: the mass of cement. Formula: Vdry = 1.52 Vwet; Vc = Vdry/Σparts; C = Vcρc. Solve:

Vdry = 1.52 m3
sum of parts = 1 + 1.5 + 3 = 5.5
Vc = 1.52 / 5.5 = 0.2764 m3
C  = 0.2764 x 1440 = 398.0 kg

Answer: 3450 bricks before wastage and 3623 to order; 398 kg of cement per cubic metre of M20, which is about 8 bags.

Check: part one substitutes back — 3450 × 0.002 = 6.90 m3, the wall volume — and 3450 bricks in 6.9 m3 is about 500 bricks per cubic metre, the figure a mason carries. Part two substitutes back — 398/1440 = 0.2764 m3 and 0.2764 × 5.5 = 1.52 m3 — and independently M20 takes about 400 kg of cement per cubic metre, so 398 is within 0.5%. Each answer has one characteristic error that the anchor catches: using the moulded brick size gives 4483 bricks, about 30% too many, and omitting the dry volume factor gives 262 kg, far too little for M20.

Exam close: “Hence the brickwork quantity is the wall volume divided by the volume of one brick together with its mortar joint, and the cement quantity is the dry volume divided by the sum of the mix proportions — the two factors that between them account for every mark lost on this question.”
