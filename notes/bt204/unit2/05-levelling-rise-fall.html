Lesson · BT204 Unit 2 — Surveying & Positioning

Levelling Book — the Rise and Fall Method and the Arithmetic Check

This is the most valuable single question in BT-204 Unit 2: the staff readings to reduced levels by the Rise and Fall method, with the arithmetic check, appeared in 4 of the last 10 papers with almost identical wording, and it is a full question rather than a part. It is also the only piece of genuine computation in the unit, so it rewards practice more than anything else here.

The one-sentence statement

Levelling is the operation of determining the difference in elevation between
points, using a level to establish a horizontal line of sight and a graduated
staff to measure the vertical distance from that line to each point. The
reduced level (RL) of a point is its height above a datum, usually taken as
mean sea level. In the RISE AND FALL method the RLs are obtained by comparing
each staff reading with the one before it: if the reading falls, the ground has
RISEN, and if the reading rises, the ground has FALLEN.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “The instrument never measures a level — it measures the vertical distance from a horizontal line of sight to a staff. Everything else is the arithmetic of turning those distances into heights.”

The Rise and Fall method — how it works

The method asks one question of every consecutive pair of readings: has the staff reading gone up or down?

If the reading DECREASES, the ground has RISEN:
      rise = previous reading - current reading

If the reading INCREASES, the ground has FALLEN:
      fall = current reading - previous reading

and then

      RL = previous RL  +  rise        (or)   -  fall
Why a falling reading means rising ground

The staff stands on the ground and the instrument looks along a horizontal line. If you walk uphill, your feet get closer to that horizontal line, so the staff reading gets smaller. So a decreasing reading means rising ground. This is the one inversion in the whole topic and it is where careless answers go wrong.

The change point — the subtlety that decides the question

When the instrument is moved, an FS is taken on the change point from the old setup and a BS is taken on the same point from the new setup. Those two readings are of the same ground, but they come from two different instrument heights, so no rise or fall is computed between them.

   AT A CHANGE POINT THE RS ARE THE SAME POINT, SO NO RISE/FALL

   SETUP 1                          SETUP 2
   line at HI-1                     line at HI-2 (different!)

   =========+=========              ==========+=========
            |                                 |
            | FS = 1.050                      | BS = 1.350
            |                                 |
   ---------+=================================+---------
                 CHANGE POINT  (one point, RL unchanged)

   The difference between the two readings, 1.350 - 1.050 = 0.300,
   is the amount the INSTRUMENT LINE dropped. It is NOT the ground
   moving. So computing a "rise" or "fall" here would inject a
   0.300 m error into every RL after it.

   WHY IT IS SAFE: the change point already has its RL from the FS.
   The BS merely starts the next setup from that same RL.

   PROOF -- the arithmetic check still balances only if it is
   omitted. Include it and the check fails by exactly the value
   you wrongly inserted.
The arithmetic check, and why it proves the rule. The three quantities below must be equal, and if they are not, a reading has been entered wrongly:

ΣBS − ΣFS  =  ΣRise − ΣFall  =  last RL − first RL

This identity holds only if no rise or fall is computed at a change point. Insert a spurious 0.300 m entry there and the middle expression changes while the other two do not — so the check failing is itself the evidence that the entry does not belong. That is why the check is worth writing on every answer: it catches the mistake that costs the whole question.

Given:  the staff readings in order, with the kind of each (BS/IS/FS),
        the instrument shifts, and the RL of the starting bench mark
Find:   the RL of every point, and the arithmetic check

        RL = previous RL + rise  or  - fall

Solved A — a short run, one setup

A level is set up and readings are taken on four points from a bench mark of RL 100.000 m: BS on the BM 1.500, IS on point A 1.250, IS on point B 1.100, FS on point C 1.050. Reduce the levels by the Rise and Fall method.

Given: RL of BM = 100.000 m; readings 1.500 (BS), 1.250 (IS), 1.100 (IS), 1.050 (FS). Find: the RL of each point, and the check. Formula: rise = previous − current; RL = previous RL + rise. Solve:

Point   BS     IS     FS    Rise   Fall     RL
BM    1.500                            100.000
A            1.250          0.250      100.250     (1.500-1.250)
B            1.100          0.150      100.400     (1.250-1.100)
C                   1.050   0.050      100.450     (1.100-1.050)
      -----  -----  -----   -----  ----
      1.500         1.050   0.450   0.000

CHECK: sum BS - sum FS = 1.500 - 1.050 = 0.450
       sum rise - sum fall = 0.450 - 0.000 = 0.450
       last RL - first RL = 100.450 - 100.000 = 0.450     ALL AGREE

Answer: RL of A = 100.250 m, of B = 100.400 m, of C = 100.450 m.

Check: all three forms of the arithmetic check give 0.450 m, so the reduction is consistent. Independent sense check: every reading became smaller as the survey progressed, so every point is a rise and there are no falls at all — which is what the table shows, and the ground is therefore climbing steadily away from the benchmark. Had a fall appeared in the table where the readings clearly decreased, it would immediately signal a sign error.

Solved B — a full run with two instrument shifts

A levelling run starts from a bench mark of RL 110.200 m. Readings are taken at 20 m intervals, and the instrument is shifted after the 4th and the 8th readings. The readings in order are:

1  BS 1.585   2  IS 1.420   3  IS 1.310   4  FS 1.225
5  BS 1.480   6  IS 1.615   7  IS 1.745   8  FS 1.860
9  BS 1.420  10  IS 1.290  11  FS 1.155

Reduce the levels and apply the arithmetic check.

Given: BM RL = 110.200 m; the eleven readings above; shifts after readings 4 and 8. Find: the RL of every point, and the arithmetic check. Formula: rise = previous − current; RL = previous RL + rise; no rise/fall at a change point. Solve:

Stn        BS      IS      FS     Rise    Fall      RL      Remarks
BM       1.585                                   110.200
1               1.420             0.165         110.365
2               1.310             0.110         110.475
3                       1.225     0.085         110.560   CHANGE POINT
4        1.480                                  110.560   instrument moved
5               1.615                     0.135 110.425
6               1.745                     0.130 110.295
7                       1.860             0.115 110.180   CHANGE POINT
8        1.420                                  110.180   instrument moved
9               1.290             0.130         110.310
10                      1.155     0.135         110.445
        ------  ------  ------   ------  ------
         4.485           4.240    0.625   0.380

CHECK: sum BS - sum FS = 4.485 - 4.240 = 0.245
       sum rise - sum fall = 0.625 - 0.380 = 0.245
       last RL - first RL = 110.445 - 110.200 = 0.245     ALL THREE AGREE

Answer: the reduced levels run 110.200, 110.365, 110.475, 110.560, 110.560, 110.425, 110.295, 110.180, 110.180, 110.310 and 110.445 m at the last point.

Check: all three forms give 0.245 m. Note what the table shows at the change points: stations 3 and 7 take an FS, and the BS on stations 4 and 8 is taken on the same ground, so their RLs are identical to the row above and they carry no rise and no fall. That is not a shortcut, it is the rule, and the arithmetic check proves it — inserting the 1.480 − 1.225 = 0.255 m or the 1.420 − 1.860 = −0.440 m differences as a rise or a fall would break all three forms of the check simultaneously. A second, independent sense check: the run starts at 110.200 and ends at 110.445, so the ground is 0.245 m higher at the end than at the start, and the falls that occur in the middle (−0.135, −0.130, −0.115 between stations 5 and 7) are more than offset by the rises. The profile therefore dips and recovers, which is exactly what the rise and fall columns show.

Solved C — finding a reading from the check

A run is reduced and the last fore sight is illegible. The readings are: BS 1.500 on a bench mark of RL 100.000; IS 1.250; FS 1.100; BS 1.350; IS 1.500; and the final FS, which is unreadable. The RL of the final point is known to be 100.100 m. Find the missing reading.

Given: BM RL = 100.000 m; the readings above; final RL = 100.100 m. Find: the missing FS. Formula: the arithmetic check, ΣBS − ΣFS = last RL − first RL. Solve:

Let the missing FS be x.

sum BS = 1.500 + 1.350 = 2.850
sum FS = 1.100 + x

check:  2.850 - (1.100 + x) = 100.100 - 100.000
        1.750 - x           = 0.100
        x                   = 1.650 m

VERIFY by continuing the reduction:
RL after BS 1.350        = 100.400   (change point, same as above)
IS 1.500 -> fall 0.150   = 100.250
FS 1.650 -> fall 0.150   = 100.100   matches the given final RL

Answer: the missing fore sight is 1.650 m.

Check: the two routes agree. The check gave x = 1.650 from the sums alone, and continuing the reduction row by row from the change point reaches exactly the given final RL of 100.100 m — 100.400, then a fall of 0.150 to 100.250, then a further fall of 0.150 to 100.100. That the two independent routes meet is the confirmation. Note also what this question proves about the check: it can recover a BS or an FS, because those are the only readings that enter the sums. An intermediate sight cannot be recovered this way, because IS readings do not appear in ΣBS or ΣFS at all — they cancel out of the check entirely, which is worth one line in any answer about the method's limitations.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • No rise or fall at a change point. The FS and the following BS are the same ground point. Inserting a rise or fall there injects the change in instrument height into every later RL.
  • A decreasing reading means RISING ground. The most common inversion in the topic: bigger reading, lower ground.
  • Rise is previous minus current; fall is current minus previous. Writing both as “the difference” loses the sign and therefore the direction.
  • Write all three forms of the check. ΣBS − ΣFS, ΣRise − ΣFall, and last RL − first RL. One of them alone proves nothing about the other two columns.
  • An IS can never be recovered from the check. Intermediate sights do not appear in ΣBS or ΣFS, so they cancel out.
  • The BS is on a point of known RL; the FS is on a point of unknown RL. Swapping which is which reverses the whole table.
  • Every setup must end with an FS. An instrument cannot be moved without one, because the new setup has no reference otherwise.
  • Keep three decimals throughout. Levelling numerals are given to the millimetre, and rounding to two places will break the check by a millimetre or two.
Special lines for extra marks
  • Opening line — “Levelling is the operation of determining the difference in elevation between points by means of a level and a graduated staff, and the reduced level of a point is its height above an assumed datum such as mean sea level.”
  • Back sight line — “A back sight is the first reading taken after the instrument is set up, on a point of known reduced level; a fore sight is the last reading taken before the instrument is moved, on a point whose level is to be determined.”
  • Rise and Fall line — “In the rise and fall method the difference between each staff reading and the one preceding it is calculated; if the reading decreases the ground has risen and if it increases the ground has fallen, and the reduced level is obtained by adding the rise or subtracting the fall from the preceding reduced level.”
  • Change point line — “No rise or fall is computed at a change point, because the fore sight and the following back sight are readings of the same point taken from two different instrument heights, so their difference is a change in the height of the instrument and not a change in the level of the ground.”
  • Check line — “The arithmetic check is that the difference of the sums of the back sights and fore sights, the difference of the sums of the rises and falls, and the difference of the last and first reduced levels must all be equal.”
  • Closing line — “Hence the rise and fall method is preferred to the height of collimation method because it provides a complete arithmetic check on every reading rather than only on the reduced levels of the change points.”
Exam tips
  • Draw the table first with the column headings before entering a single number
  • Mark the change points as you go — they are where the marks are lost
  • Work one row at a time, top to bottom, never jumping ahead
  • Show the subtraction in the rise or fall column, in brackets, so the examiner can see where the number came from
  • Write all three check lines and box the value they agree on
  • State the RLs as a list at the end so the answer is readable without the table
  • Never leave blank — the table structure plus the first three rows already clears several marks

The Rise and Fall Method — Full Revision

Statement to write verbatim

LEVELLING is the operation of determining the difference in elevation between
points by means of a level and a graduated staff. The REDUCED LEVEL (RL) of a
point is its height above an assumed datum, usually mean sea level.

READINGS
  BS  first reading after the instrument is set up, on a KNOWN RL
  IS  any reading between the BS and the FS
  FS  last reading before the instrument is moved, on an UNKNOWN RL
  CP  the point taking an FS and then the next BS

RISE AND FALL METHOD
  decreasing reading  ->  RISE = previous - current       RL goes UP
  increasing reading  ->  FALL = current - previous       RL goes DOWN
  RL = previous RL + rise   or   - fall

  AT A CHANGE POINT: NO RISE OR FALL IS TAKEN.
  The FS and the next BS are the same ground point read from two
  different instrument heights, so their difference is instrument
  movement, not ground movement.

ARITHMETIC CHECK -- all three must agree:
  sum BS - sum FS  =  sum Rise - sum Fall  =  last RL - first RL

Derivation in four steps

1. Tabulate   -> columns: Stn | BS | IS | FS | Rise | Fall | RL | Remarks
2. Compare    -> each reading against the PREVIOUS one
                 previous - current  = rise  (positive)
                 current - previous  = fall  (positive)
                 SKIP at a change point
3. Accumulate -> RL = previous RL + rise  or  - fall
4. Check      -> three sums must be equal; write all three

Levelling operations, one line each. Profile levelling — along a route, to give the section of a road, canal or railway. Cross-sectional — at right angles to the route, to give the width and shape of the ground. Differential — to find the difference in level between two or more points. Fly levelling — a rapid run, usually to transfer a level from a far benchmark to the site.

Definitions to reproduce

  • Levelling — determining the difference in elevation between points by means of a level and a graduated staff.
  • Reduced level — the height of a point above the assumed datum.
  • Bench mark — a permanently fixed point of known reduced level, used to start or close a run.
  • Back sight — the first reading taken after setting up, on a point of known reduced level.
  • Fore sight — the last reading taken before moving the instrument, on a point of unknown reduced level.
  • Intermediate sight — any reading between the back sight and the fore sight of the same setup.
  • Change point — a point on which a fore sight is taken from one setup and the following back sight from the next, so that the reduced level is carried forward.
  • Height of instrument — the reduced level of the line of sight, equal to the reduced level of the point plus the back sight on it.
  • Line of collimation — the horizontal line of sight of the level.
  • Rise and fall method — a method of reduction in which consecutive readings are compared, the difference being a rise if the reading decreases and a fall if it increases.
  • Fly levelling — a rapid levelling run carried out only to transfer a reduced level from one point to another.

Numeral shortcut

One rule and one omission. The rule: decreasing reading, rise; increasing reading, fall, and the RL moves the same way as the ground. The omission: no rise or fall at a change point, because the FS and the next BS are the same ground read from two instrument heights. Finish with all three check lines — ΣBS − ΣFS = ΣRise − ΣFall = last RL − first RL — and box the value they agree on. If two of the three disagree, look first at the change-point rows.

Given: BM RL = 110.200 m; the eleven readings; instrument shifted after readings 4 and 8. Find: the RL of every point and the arithmetic check.

Method. The first reading is a back sight on the benchmark, which fixes the starting RL. Each following reading is compared with the one before it: where the reading decreases the ground has risen, and the rise is the previous reading minus the current one; where it increases the ground has fallen, and the fall is the current reading minus the previous one. At stations 3 and 7 a fore sight is taken, and stations 4 and 8 then take a back sight on the same ground from the new instrument position, so their reduced levels repeat the row above and no rise or fall is computed for them.

Reduced levels. 110.200 m (BM), 110.365, 110.475, 110.560 (change point), 110.560, 110.425, 110.295, 110.180 (change point), 110.180, 110.310, and 110.445 m at the last point.

Check. All three forms agree at 0.245 m. The agreement is meaningful rather than coincidental: the first form uses only the back sights and fore sights, the second uses only the rise and fall columns, and the third uses only the reduced-level column — so three independently filled columns producing the same number verifies all of them at once. Note on the change points: the 1.480 − 1.225 = 0.255 m and 1.420 − 1.860 = −0.440 m differences are the amounts the instrument line changed height between setups, not movements of the ground, which is why they are omitted; inserting either one would break all three checks simultaneously.

Exam close: “Hence the reduced levels are obtained by accumulating the rises and falls along the run, and the arithmetic check confirms the reduction because the difference of the sums of the back sights and fore sights, the difference of the sums of the rises and falls, and the difference of the last and first reduced levels are all equal to 0.245 m.”

Model answer · 5 marks

Define back sight, fore sight and intermediate sight. What is a change point, and why is no rise or fall taken at it?

Four short definitions, then the reasoning. The reasoning is the same insight that protects the numerical, so it is worth having precisely.

   ONE SETUP
                LINE OF COLLIMATION (horizontal)
   =====+===============+===============+===============+========= HI
        |               |               |               |
        | BS            | IS            | IS            | FS
        |               |               |               |
   -----+---------+-----+---------+-----+---------+-----+---------
      BM A       pt 1            pt 2            CHANGE POINT
      known RL                                      carries the RL
                                                    into the next setup

   ACROSS THE SHIFT -- THE SAME POINT TAKES BOTH
   SETUP 1                          SETUP 2
   =========+=========              ==========+=========
            | FS = 1.050                      | BS = 1.350
            |                                 |
   ---------+=================================+---------
                 ONE GROUND POINT, ONE RL

   1.350 - 1.050 = 0.300 is the INSTRUMENT dropping, not the ground.
   So NO rise or fall is entered here.
  • Back sight (BS) — the first reading taken after the instrument is set up, taken on a point whose reduced level is already known. It establishes the height of the instrument.
  • Fore sight (FS) — the last reading taken before the instrument is moved, taken on a point whose reduced level is to be determined.
  • Intermediate sight (IS) — any reading taken between the back sight and the fore sight of the same setup.
  • Change point (CP) — a point on which a fore sight is taken from one setup and then the following back sight from the next setup, so that the reduced level is carried forward unchanged across the shift of the instrument. Every setup must end on a change point, because the new setup has no reference otherwise.

Why no rise or fall at a change point. The fore sight is taken on the change point from the old instrument position, and the following back sight is taken on the same point from the new one. Both readings are therefore of one piece of ground, but they are measured from two different heights of instrument line. Their difference is the amount the instrument changed height, not the amount the ground changed level — so computing a rise or fall from them would inject that instrument movement into every reduced level after it. The change point already has its reduced level from the fore sight, and the back sight merely starts the next setup from that same value.

Exam close: “Hence a fore sight and the following back sight at a change point are readings of the same point from two instrument heights, and their difference represents the movement of the instrument rather than of the ground — which is why no rise or fall is entered for it, and why the arithmetic check balances only when it is omitted.”

Model answer · 5 marks

Differentiate between the height of collimation method and the Rise and Fall method. Which is preferred and why?

A four-row comparison ending on a recommendation with its reason. The deciding difference is how complete the arithmetic check is.

   SAME READINGS, TWO METHODS

   HEIGHT OF COLLIMATION                RISE AND FALL
   ====================                 =============
   HI = RL + BS, then                   compare each reading
   RL = HI - reading                    with the previous one

   Stn  BS   IS   FS   HI    RL         Stn  Rise  Fall   RL
   BM  1.500          101.500 100.000   BM               100.000

   C             1.050   -     100.450  C    0.050        100.450

   one subtraction per point            a subtraction AND an
   (HI computed once per setup)          addition per point

     (intermediate sights are not         sum BS - sum FS =
      checked at all)                     sum Rise - sum Fall =
                                          last RL - first RL
PointHeight of collimationRise and Fall
How the RL is foundCompute the height of instrument as HI = RL + BS, then subtract each reading from itCompare each reading with the preceding one to get a rise or a fall, then add it to or subtract it from the preceding RL
Arithmetic checkPossible only on the reduced levels of the change points; the intermediate sights are not checked at allA complete check on every reading: ΣBS − ΣFS = ΣRise − ΣFall = last RL − first RL

Which is preferred, and why. The rise and fall method is preferred, because its arithmetic check covers every reading in the run, whereas the height of collimation method checks only the reduced levels of the change points. In the collimation method an error in an intermediate sight — a misread, or a digit transposed — passes through the entire reduction undetected; in the rise and fall method the same error breaks the check immediately. The cost is a little more arithmetic, which is a small price for a method that cannot silently carry a mistake into the results.

Exam close: “Hence the two methods differ chiefly in the completeness of their checks rather than in their accuracy, and the rise and fall method is preferred because it verifies every reading instead of only the change points — at the cost of one extra addition per point.”
