Lesson · BT204 Unit 2 — Surveying & Positioning

Surveying Fundamentals — Principles, Chain Surveying, Corrections and EDM

This is the foundation unit for everything else in BT-204 Unit 2. RGPV asks it directly as “what is surveying and what are its principles” (2 of 10 papers), and it is the vocabulary every levelling and traversing question assumes. The chain corrections are the only numerals here, and they are the same three moves every time: slope, temperature, and a tape of the wrong length.

The one-sentence statement

Surveying is the art and science of determining the relative positions of
points on, above or below the surface of the earth, by measuring the
horizontal and vertical distances between them, the angles between them, and
the directions of the lines joining them, and then presenting the result to a
suitable scale in the form of a map or plan. Its two governing principles are
to WORK FROM THE WHOLE TO THE PART, and to LOCATE A POINT BY MEASURING AT
LEAST TWO INDEPENDENT QUANTITIES to it from known points.

Then the framing sentence that makes the rest of the page logical: “Surveying is two jobs, not one: measurement, in which you take the field readings, and network design, in which you decide which readings to take so that an error cannot hide. The two principles below are both about the second job.”

The two principles — this is the examined part

PRINCIPLE 1 -- WORK FROM THE WHOLE TO THE PART

     WRONG: build up from small pieces        RIGHT: fix the frame first,
            (errors accumulate)               then infill (errors localised)

     c----d                                   A-------------------B
     |    |                                     |   .           |
     a----b                                     |       .       |
        errors add end to end                    |   .       .   |
     so the far corner drifts                     D-------------------C
        ...............
                                                  frame ABCD surveyed first
                                                  and checked; detail fills in

  Why: a small error in a small triangle is a LARGE error in the far corner.
  Fixing a big accurate frame first, and filling in detail afterwards, keeps
  every detail error local instead of letting it accumulate.

PRINCIPLE 2 -- LOCATE A POINT BY TWO MEASUREMENTS

     a single distance only says the point
     is SOMEWHERE on a circle          ->  ambiguous

     + -------------------------- P1
              \                  /
               \                /        two distances from two
                \              /         known points fix it
                 \            /          uniquely
                  \          /
              A ----------- B
                   BASE LINE

  So every point is fixed by TWO independent quantities -- two distances
  (chain surveying) or one distance and one angle (theodolite traversing).

Chain surveying — the terms the questions use

Check line against tie line. This pair is asked directly, and the words sound interchangeable. A check line exists only to detect error — if it disagrees with what the framework predicts, a mistake has been made. A tie line is a working measurement that fixes a point, or carries a measurement across an obstacle. A check line proves; a tie line locates.

Chain corrections — the three numerals

A chain is correct only under the conditions it was standardised for. Everything else needs a correction, and all three corrections below are for length.

Slope (or hypotenusal)Cs = h2 / 2L, where h is the difference in level between the endsAlways subtracted — the sloping distance is longer than the horizontal one
TemperatureCt = α (Tm − T0) LAdded when measured hotter than standard, subtracted when colder
Standardisation (wrong tape length)True length = measured × (actual length / nominal length)Added if the tape is too long, subtracted if too short

Solved A — slope correction

A line measured along sloping ground is 20.00 m long, and the difference in level between its two ends is 2.00 m. Find the correction for slope and the true horizontal length.

Given: L = 20.00 m, h = 2.00 m. Find: Cs and the horizontal length. Formula: Cs = h2/2L. Solve:

Cs = (2.00)^2 / (2 x 20.00)
   = 4.00 / 40.00
   = 0.10 m

horizontal length = 20.00 - 0.10 = 19.90 m

Answer: the slope correction is 0.10 m, and the true horizontal length is 19.90 m.

Check: substituting back, 19.90 + 0.10 = 20.00 m, recovering the measured length. Independent check by the exact formula: h/L = 0.10, so θ = 5.74° and the exact horizontal length is 20.00 × cos 5.74° = 19.900 m — agreeing with the approximation to three decimal places, which confirms both the arithmetic and that the small-angle approximation is valid at this slope. On the sign: the correction must be subtracted, because a distance measured along sloping ground is always longer than its horizontal projection. An answer of 20.10 m would mean the correction had been added, which is physically impossible.

Solved B — temperature correction

A 30.00 m steel tape was standardised at 20°C. A line is measured with it on a day when the tape temperature is 35°C, and the measured length is 30.00 m. Taking α = 0.0000112 per °C, find the temperature correction and the true length.

Given: L = 30.00 m, T0 = 20°C, Tm = 35°C, α = 0.0000112 per °C. Find: Ct and the true length. Formula: Ct = α(Tm − T0)L. Solve:

Ct = 0.0000112 x (35 - 20) x 30.00
   = 0.0000112 x 15 x 30.00
   = 0.00504 m

true length = 30.00 + 0.00504 = 30.00504 m

Answer: the temperature correction is +0.00504 m, giving a true length of 30.005 m.

Check: substituting back, 0.00504/(0.0000112 × 30.00) = 15°C, recovering the temperature difference. On the sign: the tape is hotter than standard, so it has expanded, so each tape length laid down is slightly longer than 30 m, so the distance measured is slightly too short — and the correction must be added. Reversing that reasoning is the standard error, so it is worth stating on the page: hot tape, add; cold tape, subtract. The magnitude also passes a sense check, since 5 mm in 30 m is about 1 part in 6000, which is the right order for a 15°C change in steel.

Solved C — a tape of the wrong length

A line is measured as 150.00 m using a tape nominally 30 m long, which is later found to be actually 30.005 m long. Find the true length of the line. State also whether the error in the tape is a cumulative or a compensating error.

Given: measured length ML = 150.00 m; nominal length ln = 30.000 m; actual length la = 30.005 m. Find: the true length, and the class of the error. Formula: TL = ML × la/ln. Solve:

TL = 150.00 x (30.005 / 30.000)
   = 150.00 x 1.0001667
   = 150.025 m

error per tape length = 30.005 - 30.000 = +0.005 m
number of tape lengths laid = 150.00 / 30.000 = 5
total error = 5 x 0.005 = +0.025 m   ->  150.000 + 0.025 = 150.025 m

Answer: the true length is 150.025 m. The error is cumulative, because the same error of 0.005 m is repeated on every tape length laid down, so it accumulates in proportion to the number of settings.

Check: the two routes agree exactly — the ratio method gives 150.025 m and the accumulation method gives 150.000 + 0.025 = 150.025 m. That agreement is itself the check, because it proves the error scales with the number of tape lengths, which is precisely what “cumulative” means. Second check on sign and size: the tape is too long, so each length it reports as 30 m is really slightly more, so the true distance must be larger than the measured one — and 150.025 is larger than 150.000. An answer below 150 m would mean the tape had been found too short.

Numeral traps in this topic
  • Slope correction is always subtracted. Sloping ground always over-measures, so the correction can never be added.
  • Hot tape, add; cold tape, subtract. A tape hotter than standard has expanded, so it under-reads, so the true distance is longer.
  • h is the difference in level, not the slope length. In Cs = h2/2L, the h is the rise between the ends and the L is the measured sloping length.
  • The temperature correction needs α in per °C and a temperature difference, not an absolute temperature. Using 35 instead of 15 gives more than twice the answer.
  • A tape too long means a true length larger than measured. Ratio it the right way up: TL = ML × actual / nominal.
  • Corrections are always small. A correction of a metre in a 30 m line means a formula or a unit has gone wrong — α is per °C, not per 100°C.
  • Naming the error class: a tape of wrong length gives a cumulative error; a mistake in reading a single chain gives a compensating one.
  • Check line against tie line. A check line only detects error; a tie line fixes position. They are not interchangeable.

Definitions to reproduce

  • Surveying — the art and science of determining the relative positions of points on the earth's surface by measuring distances and angles, and presenting the result to scale.
  • Plane surveying — surveying in which the curvature of the earth is ignored and the surface treated as a plane.
  • Geodetic surveying — surveying in which the curvature of the earth is taken into account.
  • Base line — the longest and most carefully measured main line of a chain survey.
  • Check line — a line measured only to verify that no mistake has been made in the framework.
  • Tie line — a line joining two known points, used to fix a station or to check or carry a measurement past an obstacle.
  • Offset — the lateral distance measured from a chain line to a detail.
  • Ranging — the process of fixing intermediate points on a straight line between two stations.
  • EDM — electronic distance measurement; determining distance from the time or phase difference of a modulated electromagnetic wave returned from a reflector.
  • Cumulative error — an error that is repeated on every setting and so accumulates in proportion to the number of settings; the wrong length of a tape is the standard example.
  • Compensating error — an error that is as likely to be positive as negative and therefore tends to cancel over many observations.

Model answer · 5 marks

What is EDM? State its principle and its advantages over chaining.

The principle is a time or phase measurement of a modulated wave; the advantages each answer a specific weakness of the chain.

   EDM -- ELECTRONIC DISTANCE MEASUREMENT
   ======================================

        INSTRUMENT                              REFLECTOR
        (at A)                                  (at B, a prism)
           |                                        |
           |  --------- modulated wave ---------->   |
           |                                        |
           |  <--------- returned wave ----------   |
           |                                        |
        measures the TIME taken, or more usually
        the PHASE DIFFERENCE between the outgoing
        and the returning wave

                        |
                        v
        D = (v x t) / 2        v = velocity of the wave
                               /2 because it travels there AND back

        or by phase:   D = (n x lambda + delta) / 2
                       lambda = wavelength of the modulation

   WHY IT BEATS THE CHAIN:
     no ranging needed over long lines
     ground may be undulating, wooded or built over
     a single measurement, not many tape lengths
     accuracy independent of temperature, slope and tension

What it is. EDM, electronic distance measurement, is the determination of the distance between two stations by electronic means rather than by laying a chain or tape along the ground.

Principle. A modulated electromagnetic wave — light, infrared or microwave — is transmitted from the instrument at one station to a reflector or a transponder at the other, and returned. The distance is obtained either from the time the wave takes to travel to the reflector and back, the distance being half the product of the velocity and the time, or, more usually and more accurately, from the phase difference between the outgoing and the returning wave, resolved against the known wavelengths of the modulation.

Advantages over chaining.

  • Speed. One measurement replaces many tape lengths laid end to end.
  • Range. Lines of kilometres can be measured in a single setting, where a chain would need continuous ranging.
  • Independence of ground conditions. The line need not be walkable — rivers, ravines, traffic and vegetation do not obstruct the measurement.
  • Accuracy. The result does not depend on temperature, slope, tension or sag, so the corrections that dominate chain surveying largely disappear.
  • Fewer errors. There is no cumulative error from repeatedly laying a tape, which is the characteristic error of chaining.

Exam close: “Hence EDM replaces the physical laying of a chain by a measurement of the travel of a modulated wave, which removes at once the cumulative error of the chain, the need for ranging, and the dependence of the result on the slope, temperature and tension of the tape.”

Model answer · 5 marks

A line on sloping ground is 20 m with a 2 m difference in level. A 30 m tape standardised at 20°C is used at 35°C. Find both corrections.

One line of arithmetic each, and both marks are in the sign. State the rule before substituting.

   CORRECTION 1 -- SLOPE                 CORRECTION 2 -- TEMPERATURE
   =====================                 ==========================

   L = 20.00 m  (along the ground)       tape standardised at 20 C
   h =  2.00 m  (difference in level)    used at 35 C  ->  HOTTER

   Cs = h^2 / (2L)                       Ct = alpha (Tm - To) L
      = 4.00 / 40.00                       = 0.0000112 x (35-20) x 30
      = 0.10 m                             = 0.0000112 x 15 x 30
                                           = 0.00504 m
   ALWAYS SUBTRACTED
   because ground distance is             ADDED
   ALWAYS longer than horizontal          because a HOT tape has EXPANDED,
                                          so each 30 m laid is really more,
   horizontal = 20.00 - 0.10               so the distance is UNDER-read,
              = 19.90 m                   so the true length is LONGER

                                          true length = 30.00 + 0.00504
                                                      = 30.005 m

   THE TWO SIGNS ARE THE WHOLE QUESTION:
     slope       -> always subtract    (never physically possible to add)
     hot tape    -> add                (expanded tape under-reads)
     cold tape   -> subtract           (contracted tape over-reads)

Part one — slope. Given: L = 20.00 m, h = 2.00 m. Find: Cs. Formula: Cs = h2/2L. Solve:

Cs = 4.00 / 40.00 = 0.10 m,  SUBTRACTED
horizontal length = 20.00 - 0.10 = 19.90 m

Part two — temperature. Given: L = 30.00 m, T0 = 20°C, Tm = 35°C, α = 0.0000112 per °C. Find: Ct. Formula: Ct = α(Tm − T0)L. Solve:

Ct = 0.0000112 x 15 x 30.00 = 0.00504 m,  ADDED
true length = 30.00 + 0.00504 = 30.005 m

Answer: slope correction 0.10 m subtracted, giving a horizontal length of 19.90 m; temperature correction 0.00504 m added, giving a true length of 30.005 m.

Check: the slope result substitutes back, 19.90 + 0.10 = 20.00 m, and checked exactly, h/L = 0.10 gives θ = 5.74° and 20.00 cos 5.74° = 19.900 m, agreeing with the approximation to three decimals. The temperature result substitutes back, 0.00504/(0.0000112 × 30.00) = 15°C, recovering the difference. Both magnitudes pass a sense check: 0.10 m in 20 m is 1 part in 200, and 0.005 m in 30 m is 1 part in 6000, both the right order. On the signs — the slope correction can never be added, because sloping ground always over-measures; and a hot tape must be added, because an expanded tape under-reads the distance.

Exam close: “Hence the slope correction is subtracted because the ground distance always exceeds the horizontal, and the temperature correction is added because a tape warmer than its standard has expanded and therefore under-reads — so the two corrections act in opposite directions and each must be signed before it is applied.”
